# What the research says

This page is the source for the site's "Why this works" page and for the evidence badge on every
score and suggestion. It was compiled on 2026-10-04 from the research notes in
[docs/research/](research/). Numbers marked *(to confirm)* were found only in secondary sources
and must be checked against the paper before they appear on the site.

## The honest bottom line

Philadelphia is the best studied city in the world on this question. Two randomized trials here
found that fixing neglected places reduces nearby gun violence: cleaning and greening vacant lots,
and repairing the doors and windows of abandoned houses. Both are cheap, and in both trials the
violence did not simply move next door. The greening trial found its effect in neighborhoods below
the poverty line, and the house repair trial took place in low income neighborhoods.

Evidence for street lighting is also good. Evidence for trees is real but depends on placement.
Evidence that murals, public art, walkability, or population density reduce violence does not exist
yet. Those things matter for other reasons (beauty, culture, health, street life), so Placekeepers
shows them in their own lenses and never mixes them into the violence score.

When other cities copied demolition and cleanup programs, results were uneven: some found modest
benefits, some found nothing, and one found slightly more crime where demolition was heaviest. So
the Philadelphia results are real, but they are not a law of nature.

## Evidence badges used on the map

| Badge | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Strong** | A randomized trial, ideally in Philadelphia, found the effect. |
| **Moderate** | Careful before and after comparisons with control areas found the effect. |
| **Mixed** | Good studies disagree, or the effect depends on context. |
| **Weak** | Only perception surveys or correlations, or one small study. |
| **Not about violence** | The intervention is valuable for other reasons; no violence claim is made. |

## Greening vacant lots: Strong

**The citywide trial** (Branas and colleagues, *PNAS* 2018,
[doi:10.1073/pnas.1718503115](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1718503115)). 541 randomly chosen
Philadelphia vacant lots were randomly assigned to greening (trash removal, grading, grass, a few
trees, a low wooden post and rail fence, regular mowing), cleanup only, or nothing, and followed for
38 months. In neighborhoods **below the poverty line**, greened lots saw gun violence down 29.1%,
all crime down 13.3%, burglary down 21.9%, and nuisance crimes down 30.3%. Neighbors of greened lots
reported 36.8% less perceived crime, 57.8% fewer safety concerns about going outside, and 75.7% more
time relaxing and socializing outdoors. (Abstract checked against Europe PMC on 2026-10-04.)

**Shootings specifically** (Moyer and colleagues, *AJPH* 2019,
[doi:10.2105/AJPH.2018.304752](https://doi.org/10.2105/AJPH.2018.304752)), same trial: shootings
down 6.8% near greened lots (95% confidence interval 2.7% to 10.6%) and down 9.2% near lots that were
only cleaned and mowed (4.8% to 13.2%). No sign that shootings moved to nearby blocks.

**Mental health** (South and colleagues, *JAMA Network Open* 2018,
[doi:10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2018.0298](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2018.0298)):
neighbors of greened lots reported feeling depressed 41.5% less than the control group, and 68.7%
less among residents below the poverty line.

**Independent rating.** The US Department of Justice clearinghouse CrimeSolutions rates the PHS
vacant lot greening program "Promising" (its second tier), based on this body of work.

**Cost.** The trial's cost was about $5 per square meter to treat and $0.50 per square meter per
year to maintain. News reports put a typical lot at about $1,100 to $1,500 up front and $150 to $300
a year *(to confirm with PHS)*. PHS LandCare maintains roughly 12,000 lots, about a third of the
city's vacant land *(to confirm with PHS)*.

**Elsewhere.** Flint's community maintained lots (Heinze and colleagues 2018, observational) had
about 40% fewer assaults on their streets, though groups that organize may differ in other ways. New
Orleans found no change in violent crime, only a drop in drug crime near treated lots. Youngstown and
Chicago's Large Lots program were studied, but we have not confirmed which way their results went.

**What it means on the map.** An untreated vacant lot in a high poverty area with recent shootings
nearby is the strongest violence reduction opportunity Placekeepers can show.

## Fixing abandoned houses: Strong

**The citywide trial** (South and colleagues, *JAMA Internal Medicine* 2023,
[doi:10.1001/jamainternmed.2022.5460](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2022.5460)). 258
abandoned houses in 63 clusters, mostly in Black, low income neighborhoods, were randomly assigned to
full remediation (working doors and windows plus cleanup), cleanup only, or nothing. Full remediation
cut gun assaults by 13.1% and weapons violations by 8.4%. Shootings fell 7.0%, which was not
statistically significant. Cleanup alone did nothing measurable. No displacement found.

**The Doors and Windows ordinance** (Kondo and colleagues, *PLOS ONE* 2015,
[doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0129582](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0129582)): enforcing the
city rule that abandoned buildings have working doors and windows was followed by drops in assaults
and gun assaults, but drug and property crime rose near remediated buildings.

**Demolition elsewhere is mixed.** Baltimore found small real drops (violent crime down 3.0%,
assaults down 4.9%). Rochester found shootings down about 14% per demolition the following year.
Detroit found no effect. Cleveland found mostly nothing, and slightly more crime where demolition
was heaviest in high vacancy areas. Placekeepers therefore recommends sealing and repair, not
demolition, as the evidence backed action.

## Street lighting: Moderate

A Campbell Collaboration review of 13 studies (Welsh and Farrington 2008,
[doi:10.4073/csr.2008.13](https://doi.org/10.4073/csr.2008.13)) found crime down about 20% after
lighting improvements. Interestingly, daytime crime fell too, which suggests part of the effect is
neighbors seeing visible investment, not only better visibility. A randomized trial in New York City
public housing (Chalfin and colleagues, *Journal of Quantitative Criminology* 2022,
[doi:10.1007/s10940-020-09490-6](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10940-020-09490-6)) found a significant drop
in nighttime outdoor crime *(size to confirm; secondary sources say about a third)*. Philadelphia
does not publish streetlight locations; outage reports to 311 are the closest signal.

## Trees and green space: Mixed

Street trees are associated with less crime. In Portland, small trees on private lots that block
sightlines were associated with *more* crime, while big trees were associated with less (Donovan and
Prestemon 2012, [doi:10.1177/0013916510383238](https://doi.org/10.1177/0013916510383238)).
Placekeepers suggests trees for shade and health, placed so they keep sightlines open, and counts
canopy only as a secondary factor.

## Murals and public art: Not about violence

Mural Arts Philadelphia's Porch Light program was evaluated by Yale researchers (Tebes and
colleagues): residents reported more collective efficacy, better looking blocks, and less stigma
around mental illness. That evaluation was not peer reviewed and did not measure crime. No study
we found links murals or public art to less violence. Art belongs in the placemaking lens, and it is
welcome there.

## Walkability, busy streets, and density: Mixed, or no evidence

Research on walkability and "eyes on the street" is correlational and points both ways, partly
because the shops that make a street walkable can also include alcohol outlets, which are a
consistent risk factor. Population density has no causal evidence at all; Placekeepers uses it only
to count how many people an improvement would reach.

## Transit: Promising, but context specific

In Medellin, Colombia, neighborhoods connected by the Metrocable aerial transit line, with new public
spaces built around it, saw homicide fall 66% more than comparable neighborhoods (Cerda and
colleagues, *American Journal of Epidemiology* 2012,
[doi:10.1093/aje/kwr428](https://doi.org/10.1093/aje/kwr428); rate ratio 0.33, 95% CI 0.18 to 0.61).
Medellin's circumstances were very different from Philadelphia's. Evidence about shelters and lighting
at bus stops specifically is thin; transit comfort is its own lens.

## Street safety quick builds

**Asphalt art.** The Bloomberg Philanthropies Asphalt Art Safety Study (2022, 17 sites, before and
after) found crashes involving pedestrians and cyclists down 50%, injury crashes down 37%, and drivers
yielding immediately 27% more often. It was funded by the program's sponsor and had no randomized
control. In July 2025 the US Department of Transportation began urging states to remove crosswalk art
as a "distraction", and Florida ordered removals. No Pennsylvania or Philadelphia position was found
as of October 2026, so Placekeepers labels asphalt art "check with the City first".

**Daylighting.** Pennsylvania law already bans parking within 20 feet of a crosswalk at an
intersection (Title 75, section 3353). Daylighting in Philadelphia enforces an existing rule rather
than asking for a new one.

## What Placekeepers does not claim

**The citywide drop in killings.** Philadelphia recorded 562 homicides in 2021, 410 in 2023, 269 in
2024 and 222 in 2025, the fewest since 1966. Reporting credits mainly policing changes and roughly
$600 million in violence prevention funding, including community violence intervention. Greening
works block by block; it does not explain the citywide trend, and we never say it does.

**Policing.** Reviews of "disorder policing" find that fixing places and solving problems with
communities modestly reduces crime, while zero tolerance arrests for minor offenses do not, and they
widen racial disparities. Placekeepers is about caring for places, never about directing police
toward people.

## Risks we design for

**Green gentrification.** Research on LandCare greening finds nearby property values rise (about 4%
in the first year and about 13% after six years in press coverage of a 2026 Lincoln Institute paper,
*to confirm*), and more in areas that were already better off. Greening can add to displacement
pressure. Placekeepers shows a displacement watch layer and suggests pairing projects with
protections such as community land trusts and the Neighborhood Gardens Trust.

**Stigma.** A map of shootings can be read as "dangerous neighborhood" instead of "place that
deserves investment". Placekeepers shows shootings as counts on a hexagon grid by default, frames
them as where care helps most, and never ranks people or neighborhoods by danger.

## How to tell if your project worked

1. Compare with similar places you did not treat, not only with your own before and after.
2. Compare the *change* at your site with the *change* at the comparison sites over the same period.
3. Beware of picking a site right after an unusual spike. Spikes often fade on their own.
4. Shootings are rare. One site going from 3 to 1 is not proof. Pool many sites before claiming a
   percentage, and report the uncertainty.
5. Decide the follow up period in advance and make it long: at least a year. Baltimore saw nothing
   in the first three months.
6. Check the surrounding blocks too, in case problems moved.
7. Say plainly which kind of comparison you made.
