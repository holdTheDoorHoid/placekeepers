# Philadelphia Street Safety and Transit Comfort: Data, Rules, and Evidence

Research date: October 4, 2026. Every claim below is marked VERIFIED (fetched or queried directly this session) or UNVERIFIED (from training knowledge, a secondary synthesis I could not independently confirm, or simply not found). A separate NOT FOUND tag marks things I looked for and could not locate at all. A source URL is given for every claim.

## Plain language summary

Philadelphia has solid, usable, machine readable data for most of what this project needs. The city's own High Injury Network, its full PennDOT derived crash dataset, and the Police Department's fatal crash dataset are all live, queryable, and free, and I confirmed all three work by querying them directly. The Police fatal crash dataset let me pull exact fatal crash counts for 2019 through 2026 year to date, broken down by whether a pedestrian or cyclist was involved. The picture: fatal crashes spiked after 2020, stayed around 126 to 134 a year from 2021 to 2024, then dropped to 101 in 2025 and sit at 66 so far in 2026. Pedestrians are consistently the largest group of victims.

For memorials, the Bicycle Coalition of Greater Philadelphia runs the main public facing resources (TrafficVictimsPHL and a ghost bike program), but neither is a structured, downloadable dataset, and I found no official city or state policy governing roadside memorials. For quick build evidence, Bloomberg Philanthropies' 2022 Asphalt Art Safety Study is real, rigorous, and strongly positive (big drops in crashes and better driver yielding), but the federal government reversed course hard in 2025: USDOT and FHWA now treat crosswalk art and "distracting" pavement markings as a safety problem to be removed, not funded, and Florida has already ordered murals taken out. I found no Pennsylvania or Philadelphia response to that federal push. Philadelphia's permit paths for parklets, streeteries, plazas, traffic calming, block parties, and street trees are all real, documented, and mostly free or cheap, but I could not find a dedicated city permit path for painting an intersection or installing a plain sidewalk bench, which matters a lot given the federal climate.

For transit, SEPTA's GTFS feed and Indego's bike share data are live and verified. SEPTA does publish stop level ridership data. I could not get OpenStreetMap's Overpass API to answer from this environment (it refused every request with an error unrelated to my queries), so the shelter and bench tag counts requested are not available this session; I have included the exact queries to run. I also did not find a public dataset of bus shelter locations anywhere I checked.

## A. Vision Zero Philadelphia: status and crash data sources

### A1. Current status

Vision Zero Philadelphia launched in November 2016 under Mayor Kenney with a goal of zero traffic deaths by 2030 (VERIFIED, found via search of https://www.phila.gov/2020-11-13-city-releases-reimagined-vision-zero-action-plan-2025/ and confirmed in a fetch of https://gridphilly.com/blog-home/2026/03/01/philadelphias-program-to-eradicate-traffic-deaths-turns-10-activists-want-swifter-action/). In 2024, Mayor Cherelle Parker signed Executive Order 4-24 recommitting the city to Vision Zero, but pushed the elimination target back twenty years, from 2030 to 2050, after fatalities ran far above the original projections (VERIFIED, Grid Magazine, same URL).

The city is on its third action plan cycle. The original Three Year Action Plan (2017), the Vision Zero Action Plan 2025, and the current Vision Zero Action Plan 2030 are all listed on the official plans page, along with annual reports through 2024 (VERIFIED, fetched https://visionzerophl.com/plans-and-reports/). A new Five Year Action Plan and Capital Plan, covering 2025 through 2030, was due by October 31, 2025, built from a year of community surveys, pop up events, and roundtables (VERIFIED, fetched https://www.phila.gov/2025-01-03-how-to-get-involved-in-the-vision-zero-five-year-action-plan-process/). The plans page confirms the Action Plan 2030 is now the current, most recently published plan (VERIFIED, same fetch).

The High Injury Network, the 12 percent of city streets that carry about 80 percent of fatal and serious injury crashes, is the backbone of the whole program (VERIFIED, fetched https://visionzerophl.com/ and https://opendataphilly.org/datasets/vision-zero-high-injury-network/). OpenDataPhilly hosts three versions: 2017, 2020, and 2025 (VERIFIED, same fetch). I queried the 2025 version's live feature service directly: it holds 162 street segment features, and the service's own metadata timestamp shows it was last edited on December 10, 2025 (VERIFIED, queried https://services.arcgis.com/fLeGjb7u4uXqeF9q/arcgis/rest/services/high_injury_network_2025/FeatureServer/0 this session). That makes the 2025 network the latest version as of this research. The city's plans page separately references a "High Injury Network (2019 to 2023)" as the crash window the current network is built from (VERIFIED, fetched https://visionzerophl.com/plans-and-reports/), consistent with the original network having been built from 2014 to 2018 crash data (VERIFIED, same source).

I could not reconcile these two sets of numbers (the PPD figures are crash counts, not death counts, and in several years the "deaths" figure from secondary reporting is actually lower than my directly queried crash count for the same year, which should not happen if deaths were simply crashes with one or more fatalities). Treat the PPD table above as the most directly verifiable number for any given year, and the Grid Magazine and Bicycle Coalition figures as the numbers the advocacy and city communications world actually uses in public statements. A complete 2026 year to date figure from the city's own OTIS pipeline (as opposed to my raw PPD crash query) was NOT FOUND in this session.

## C. Evidence for quick build interventions, and the 2025 federal reversal

**Bloomberg Philanthropies Asphalt Art Safety Study (2022).** VERIFIED at the primary source: I fetched the actual PDF (https://assets.bbhub.io/dotorg/sites/43/2022/04/Asphalt-Art-Safety-Study.pdf) and read its text directly. Published April 2022, prepared for Bloomberg Philanthropies by the engineering firm Sam Schwartz with support from Street Plans. Two part study: a Historical Crash Analysis at 17 sites across five states (seven unsignalized intersections, seven signalized intersections, three mid block crossings), each with at least two years of before and after crash data, plus an Observational Behavior Assessment using before and after video at five U.S. sites painted in 2021. Headline results, stated as averaged before versus after crash rate changes:
- 50 percent decrease in the rate of crashes involving pedestrians or other vulnerable road users
- 37 percent decrease in the rate of crashes causing injuries
- 17 percent decrease in the total crash rate
- 25 percent decrease in pedestrian crossings that conflicted with a driver
- 27 percent increase in drivers yielding immediately to pedestrians with the right of way
- 38 percent decrease in pedestrians crossing against the signal

The report itself frames the core legal tension that later exploded in 2025: it notes that a strict reading of the 2009 Manual on Uniform Traffic Control Devices (MUTCD) treats most asphalt art as non compliant, that NACTO and the Institute of Transportation Engineers had pushed to have crosswalk art explicitly allowed in the next MUTCD revision, and that the outcome "will likely not be known until 2023." I did not find or search for later academic follow ups or critiques of this specific study within my time budget (NOT RESEARCHED); flagging this as a gap rather than guessing.

**Daylighting (keeping parking away from the corner).** Pennsylvania already has a long standing statewide rule, not a new 2024 or 2025 law: Title 75, Pennsylvania Consolidated Statutes, Section 3353 prohibits parking within 20 feet of a crosswalk at an intersection (and within 30 feet of a stop sign, yield sign, or signal), statewide, on both marked and unmarked crosswalks, with no sign or curb marking required for the restriction to apply (VERIFIED via search of the statute text, republished at https://codes.findlaw.com/pa/title-75-pacsa-vehicles/pa-csa-sect-75-3353/ and https://law.justia.com/codes/pennsylvania/2010/title-75/chapter-33/3353). Most of the "daylighting law" news from 2024 and 2025 concerns California's new AB 413, a different state; I found no comparable new Pennsylvania or Philadelphia legislative action in that window (NOT FOUND). Practically, this means painted or hardscaped daylighting treatments in Philadelphia are not establishing a new restriction, they are making an already illegal parking spot visibly and physically harder to use, which is a meaningfully different (and generally easier) legal posture than asking for a new rule.

**Curb extensions, flex posts, speed cushions, leading pedestrian intervals, and road diets.** I did not independently verify general effectiveness statistics for these countermeasures this session (an attempt to fetch FHWA's Proven Safety Countermeasures page at https://highways.dot.gov/safety/proven-safety-countermeasures returned HTTP 403 and could not be read). Treat any general claim about their crash reduction percentages as UNVERIFIED this session; a follow up fetch of that FHWA page (perhaps from a different network context) or NACTO's Urban Street Design Guide would be the right next step. What I did verify directly: Philadelphia's own speed cushion program runs on a seasonal installation window, typically May through October (VERIFIED, fetched https://www.phila.gov/services/streets-sidewalks-alleys/request-traffic-calming-for-a-residential-street/), and the city's FY26 budget proposal reportedly included 1.5 million dollars specifically for speed cushions as part of a larger Vision Zero capital package (found via search of phila.gov budget coverage; I did not independently fetch the specific budget page, so treat the dollar figure as UNVERIFIED in detail even though the general existence of a speed cushion budget line is well corroborated).

**The 2025 federal reversal on crosswalk art.** VERIFIED via search, corroborated across multiple news sources (I could not reach a primary USDOT or FHWA page directly, as highways.dot.gov blocked the fetch tool with a 403). In July 2025, U.S. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy announced the "SAFE ROADS" initiative (Safe Arterials for Everyone through Reliable Operations and Distraction reduced Strategies), directing that crosswalk and intersection markings stay "consistent and recognizable" and free of "distractions." Reporting describes both federal and at least one state DOT (Florida) now treating asphalt art inside streets, intersections, crosswalks, and roadside sidewalks as something to be removed, on the stated grounds that it distracts drivers and pedestrians and that autonomous vehicles cannot reliably distinguish art from real traffic control markings (VERIFIED via search, https://www.wgcu.org/arts-and-culture/2025-08-15/dot-directives-ban-and-order-removal-of-asphalt-art-in-streets-intersections-crosswalks-and-sidewalks and https://gobikebuffalo.org/federal-highway-administration-requests-crosswalk-art-removal/). FHWA's own stated guidance, per that same reporting, still permits "subdued colored aesthetic treatments between the legally marked transverse crosswalk lines," but only if every element of the pattern is "uniform, consistent, repetitive, and expected," with no pictographs, symbols, multiple color arrangements, or anything that could be read as communicating a message to road users. I searched specifically for a Pennsylvania or Philadelphia response to this directive and found none (NOT FOUND).

**What this means for activists who want to paint an intersection or a memorial.** This is my own reading of the evidence above, not a verified legal conclusion, and should be checked with the city before anyone paints anything. A plain, muted, geometric treatment confined strictly between the legal crosswalk lines is the closest thing to a safe harbor under the current federal guidance. A pictorial mural, a rainbow or multi color design, anything placed inside the travel lane markings themselves, or anything that could be read as a message (which would describe most memorial art) sits squarely in the category the 2025 federal directive targets, and Florida's experience shows a state DOT acting on that directive will order it removed. Painting just outside the marked crosswalk area (a median, a plaza, a closed off intersection corner) is a meaningfully different and likely safer category, though the broad wording used in the Florida directive ("streets, intersections, crosswalks and sidewalks") suggests enforcement could reach further than just the crosswalk stripes themselves. Given that no Pennsylvania or Philadelphia agency has stated a position either way, any guidance given to activists on this point should be labeled as uncertain and reconfirmed before a project goes ahead.

**SEPTA Bus Revolution / New Bus Network, status as of late 2026.** VERIFIED via search (billypenn.com, westphillylocal.com, phillyvoice.com, whyy.org, nbcphiladelphia.com, septa.org, transitforwardphilly.org). The redesign, launched in 2021 and delayed more than once, was renamed from "Bus Revolution" to the "New Bus Network" and began its first implementation phase on August 23, 2026, covering South Philadelphia, Kensington and Juniata, the Cheltenham and Lancaster Avenue corridors, and Roxborough. Three more phases are planned: around February or spring 2027 (expanded Direct Bus service, two new "SEPTA Go Zones," one new Chester County route), a third phase around June 2027, and a fourth around August 2027 (which falls into SEPTA's 2028 fiscal year). The plan overall cuts 16 routes, speeds up 21 others, adds 2 new routes, and is meant to raise the number of routes running every 15 minutes or better from 8 to 29 once fully implemented. Because this rolled out only weeks before this research (session date October 4, 2026), stop level data for routes in and after Phase 1 should be expected to be in active flux.

**SEPTA's 2025 to 2026 funding crisis.** VERIFIED via search (phillyvoice.com, spotlightpa.org, billypenn.com, economyleague.org, septa.org, greenphl.com, and a direct phila.gov article dated August 19, 2025, "City Planning for SEPTA Service Cuts and Fare Increases"). SEPTA entered 2025 facing a 213 million dollar budget deficit. Its board approved an FY26 budget that, absent new state funding, called for up to 45 percent service cuts and a 21.5 percent fare increase. A first round actually took effect in late summer 2025: roughly a 20 percent service cut (32 bus routes eliminated, 16 more shortened, reduced rail frequency) plus a fare increase. A Philadelphia judge issued a temporary injunction around August 29, 2025, blocking a further fare increase and a second round of cuts that had been set for September 1, after a legal challenge. A second, larger round of cuts (another 25 percent, five more Regional Rail lines, 18 more bus routes) had been planned for January 1, 2026. In September 2025, with no state funding deal reached, SEPTA asked PennDOT to let it redirect 394 million dollars in capital assistance funding toward operating costs for two years as a stopgap. I did not find a clear, dated statement of exactly what happened after that (whether the January 2026 cuts proceeded, were averted, or were partly reversed) within this session's search budget, so the picture for the months immediately before this research (session date October 2026) is UNVERIFIED and worth a fresh, narrowly scoped check before relying on current SEPTA stop or route data.
