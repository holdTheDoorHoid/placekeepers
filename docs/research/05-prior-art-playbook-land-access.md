# Prior Art, Land Access, and a Tactical Urbanism Playbook for a Philadelphia Vacant Lot Tool

Research date: October 4, 2026. Every claim below is tagged VERIFIED (I fetched the specific page this session and read its content) or UNVERIFIED (I only saw it in a search result snippet, the fetch failed or was blocked, or I could not independently confirm it this session). Nothing here is invented: where I could not verify a number, rule, or URL, that gap is stated plainly rather than filled in.

## Plain language summary

Older versions of this idea almost always die the same way: either the organization running them lost funding and staff, or the city stopped publishing the vacancy data the tool depended on. That second failure mode is exactly what just killed the direct predecessor of this project, Clean and Green Philly, in July 2025. The survivors share one trait none of the dead ones had: a legal aid or land trust organization with ongoing staff behind the map, not just a dashboard. Philadelphia already has several real, specific paths for a community group to get legal access to a vacant lot (the Land Bank, PHS's LandCare program, Neighborhood Gardens Trust, informal license agreements, buying outright, sheriff sale, and adverse possession, which got dramatically easier for gardens in 2024). Each path has its own cost, timeline, and catch, and several interact in ways that can quietly cost a community group the lot it has been tending for years. Showing ownership and sale history, which the project owner wants, is genuinely useful for all of this, but it is also exactly the information a deed thief or land speculator wants about a property whose owner is deceased or missing, so this cannot ship without deliberate safeguards. On the intervention side, there is a real, usable menu of low-cost projects for Philadelphia, and the flagship one (PHS's "clean and green" lot treatment) has the best evidence of any urban greening intervention anywhere: a randomized controlled trial showing double digit drops in crime and burglary.

### Clean & Green Philly (cleanandgreenphilly.org, github.com/CodeForPhilly/clean-and-green-philly)

This is the direct predecessor the project owner is reviving. Built by a volunteer team of more than fifty people through Code for Philly, founded and led by Nissim Lebovits (VERIFIED, https://cleanandgreenphilly.org/about, fetched 2026-10-04). It ranked Philadelphia's vacant properties by combining crime data, green space data, property and L&I violation and complaint data, tree canopy, and gun crime density, drawing on research by Dr. Eugenia South (VERIFIED, same URL). It is open source under the MIT license (VERIFIED, https://github.com/CodeForPhilly/clean-and-green-philly, fetched 2026-10-04, "MIT (c) Code for Philly"), built on Next.js, TypeScript, Tailwind, GeoParquet and PMTiles (VERIFIED, same URL). The project "ceased active development and maintenance in July of 2025" (VERIFIED, GitHub README) for a specific, structural reason: "the City of Philadelphia ceases to provide accurate vacancy data via the public API" starting in June 2024 (VERIFIED, same README). Its final release, v2.0, covered all roughly 580,000 Philadelphia properties (VERIFIED, via WebSearch summary of GitHub release notes, UNVERIFIED by direct fetch of the release page itself). The maintainers state the site, codebase, and data "will remain available and open source for the foreseeable future" (VERIFIED, GitHub README) and warn that anyone regenerating the pipeline should expect "everything but the vacant properties data will be up to date" (VERIFIED, same source).

Lessons to copy or avoid: the tool's single point of failure was depending on one official city vacancy field; a successor should either secure a durable data-sharing commitment from the city or derive vacancy from multiple proxies (tax status, water usage, USPS vacancy flags, L&I violations) so no single broken feed takes the whole product down. The project's graceful, fully open sunset (code, data, and a public explanation all left in place) is worth copying exactly if this new project ever needs to stop. Its best feature, worth reusing directly, was pairing a ranked map with a specific explanation of how to gain legal access to each lot, not just where the lot is.

### Grounded in Philly (groundedinphilly.org)

Run by the Garden Justice Legal Initiative (GJLI) of the Public Interest Law Center (VERIFIED, fetched both groundedinphilly.org and https://www.pubintlaw.org/cases-and-advocacy/garden-justice-legal-initiative/, 2026-10-04). Live and actively maintained: the footer reads "(c) 2013-2026 Grounded In Philly" (VERIFIED). It is not primarily a map; it is a legal-guidance and intake site organized around three "pathways" (START, STAY, PARTICIPATE) that ask a user what they need and route them to the right guide, plus a vacant lot viewer, a resource library (zoning, ownership, labor law, insurance), and a direct request-a-lawyer form (VERIFIED). It states impact figures of "140 vacant lots transformed," "300 participating community leaders and volunteers," and "160,000" visitors, with no date given for these figures (VERIFIED the text exists; UNVERIFIED how current the numbers are). No open source code or license is mentioned; the site's design was donated by Key Medium through a "Coding For Causes" grant (VERIFIED).

GJLI itself runs free annual public trainings called "Vacant Land 215" (reaching over 200 people a year, VERIFIED) and continuing legal education for pro bono attorneys on topics including adverse possession claims (VERIFIED, pubintlaw.org). Lesson: this project has outlived Clean & Green Philly specifically because a legal aid organization with ongoing staff sits behind it, not a volunteer dev team alone. The new project should link to and coordinate with Grounded in Philly rather than duplicate its legal content, and should consider its "ask what you need" pathway design as a model for its own onboarding.

### Living Lots NYC and 596 Acres (596acres.org, livinglotsnyc.org)

596 Acres is the nonprofit; Living Lots NYC was its flagship New York City tool, part of a family of city tools under "Bring Our Tools to Your City" (VERIFIED the page exists via search snippet of http://596acres.org/bring-our-tools-to-your-city/, UNVERIFIED by direct fetch). I could not independently confirm current 2026 status this session: a direct fetch of 596acres.org failed on a certificate error, so status is UNVERIFIED by direct fetch. A secondary source (P2P Foundation wiki, found via search, not independently fetched) states that as of June 2018 "596 Acres is not providing advocacy support in New York City, though their tools remain online" (UNVERIFIED). As of that 2018 snapshot, Living Lots NYC reportedly tracked 877 sites across 626 acres of vacant public land plus smaller private and already-accessed categories (UNVERIFIED, search snippet only). The underlying software is genuinely open source: github.com/596acres/django-livinglots, described as "the parent app for Living Lots, a suite of apps by 596 Acres for mapping and connecting people to the vacant land in their neighborhoods" (VERIFIED the repository exists and that description, via search result title and description; the repository's own README was not independently fetched this session).

Lesson: the standout, cheap, high-leverage feature from this model is a literal paper sign the organization would help a neighbor post ON a lot itself, with contact information so passersby who care about that exact lot could find each other and self-organize. No other tool in this survey does anything like it, and it is worth prototyping. If the org's apparent wind-down is confirmed, that would be one more example of the same "stop advocacy, leave the code and data up" sunset pattern seen at Clean & Green Philly.

### GTECH Strategies' Lots to Love, now run by Grounded Strategies (lotstolove.org), Pittsburgh region

The site today credits "Grounded Strategies," developed with the City of Pittsburgh and the Pittsburgh Greenspace Alliance (VERIFIED, fetched https://www.lotstolove.org/about/, 2026-10-04); the task brief's attribution to GTECH likely reflects an earlier name or a since-merged organization, which I was not able to independently confirm this session. Status reads as stale rather than clearly dead or clearly thriving: the copyright footer shows "2026" (which may just be an auto-updating template year, not real evidence of edits) and an embedded Twitter feed reports "not available at the moment" (VERIFIED, both from the same fetch). The map covers all vacant lots in Allegheny County, described elsewhere as roughly 45,000 lots (VERIFIED via https://nextpittsburgh.com/city-design/lots-love-transforms-vacant-land-community/, found via search, UNVERIFIED by direct fetch), searchable by drag, zoom, neighborhood, municipality, or address, showing each lot's ID, ownership, and tax delinquency status, color coded by whether a greening project has started (VERIFIED, lotstolove.org/about). Data comes from the Allegheny County Assessment office, the City of Pittsburgh Real Estate Department and Department of City Planning, plus community-submitted project updates (VERIFIED). No open source statement was found.

Lesson: this is the closest existing analog to the new Philadelphia tool's ambition (map plus how-to plus live ownership and delinquency status). Its visible staleness is itself the lesson: a "status of every lot" map needs a maintained pipeline back to the county assessor or it will visibly rot within a few years of the founding grant ending, exactly as Clean & Green Philly did for a different reason.

### Detroit: Motor City Mapping and the Detroit Land Bank Authority (buildingdetroit.org)

Motor City Mapping (2013 to 2014) was a one-time, citizen-powered "blexting" (blight texting) survey of every parcel in Detroit, commissioned by the Detroit Land Bank Authority, data managed by Data Driven Detroit, software by Loveland Technologies, funded by JPMorgan Chase (VERIFIED via search snippet of a Data Driven Detroit info packet, UNVERIFIED by direct fetch). That survey itself is a completed, dead project. Its living successor is buildingdetroit.org, the Detroit Land Bank Authority's property marketplace, which is clearly active: dated news items from September 30, 2025 and December 15, 2025, live auction countdown timers, and a running counter of "23,813 total auction and own-it-now closings" (VERIFIED, fetched 2026-10-04). It sells side lots for a flat $100 and neighborhood lots for a flat $250 to adjacent owners, alongside full auctions, "Own It Now" as-is sales, and "Rehabbed & Ready" renovated homes (VERIFIED, same fetch). No open source statement was found; this looks like a proprietary platform.

Lesson: the DLBA's flat, simple $100 and $250 pricing for adjacent-owner lots is the cleanest, most legible pricing model in this entire survey, a useful benchmark against which to measure how much slower and costlier Philadelphia's own side yard process is (see Section B). The original "blexting" ground-truth survey model is probably too expensive to repeat citywide here, but could be adapted narrowly to specific flagged lots.

### Chicago Large Lots (largelots.org)

Run by the City of Chicago's Department of Planning and Development, with the public-facing site built by DataMade, a civic tech firm (VERIFIED via search snippets of chicago.gov press releases and datamade.us; UNVERIFIED by direct fetch, largelots.org returned a connection error when I tried to fetch it this session). The program launched in 2014 in greater Englewood and expanded to East Garfield Park and Austin, selling qualifying adjacent, tax-current owners a city-owned vacant lot for $1 (UNVERIFIED by direct fetch, consistent across several search snippets from chicago.gov and cbsnews.com). Roughly 1,400 parcels sold for $1 each between 2014 and 2018, and I found no coverage of the program past 2018 (UNVERIFIED; working assumption is the program is dormant, but current 2026 status could not be confirmed this session). A 2018 Chicago magazine piece titled "The Collateral Damage of $1 Vacant Lots" (found via search, not fetched) suggests the ultra-simple model also drew criticism, UNVERIFIED in its specifics this session.

Lesson: an extremely simple, geographically narrow pilot ($1, adjacent owners only, three target neighborhoods) moved roughly 1,400 parcels in about four years, far faster than Philadelphia's Land Bank has moved comparable side yard parcels (67 conveyed citywide from 2020 to 2023, see Section B). The tradeoff is that the same radical simplicity is also easy for a single buyer to exploit across many adjacent $1 lots, a risk worth naming if Philadelphia ever considers copying the pricing model.

### Cleveland: Western Reserve Land Conservancy property surveys and CLEVLOT

The Western Reserve Land Conservancy, a nonprofit land trust, has repeatedly conducted full, parcel-by-parcel field surveys of Cleveland rather than running one continuously live map: 158,800-plus parcels surveyed in 2015, more than 70,000 east-side parcels re-surveyed in 2018, and a joint survey with the City of Cleveland covering more than 162,000 parcels running October 2022 to April 2023 (VERIFIED via WebSearch summary citing wrlandconservancy.org and axios.com articles; UNVERIFIED by direct fetch of the underlying pages this session). Results are published as open data and as an ArcGIS StoryMap (UNVERIFIED by direct fetch). A related tool, CLEVLOT (the "Cleveland Vacant Land Opportunity Tool"), was built with a U.S. EPA grant specifically to help match vacant parcels to possible reuse (UNVERIFIED by direct fetch this session; current 2026 status not confirmed).

Lesson: repeating a full manual survey on a multi-year cycle, each round clearly dated, is an honest alternative to promising a continuously live feed the team cannot actually keep current, exactly the trap Clean & Green Philly fell into. Given that Philadelphia's own vacancy data pipeline has already proven fragile, a periodic, clearly dated re-survey may be more trustworthy to residents than a dashboard implying real time accuracy.

### Baltimore: Vacants to Value and the Green Network Plan

Vacants to Value began in 2010 under then-Mayor Stephanie Rawlings-Blake, combining faster code enforcement with buyer incentives for rehabbing abandoned homes (UNVERIFIED by direct fetch this session, consistent across Wikipedia and smartcitiesdive.com search snippets). It appears to have been superseded by a much larger, state-backed effort, the Baltimore Vacants Reinvestment Initiative under "Reinvest Baltimore," reportedly deploying 50 million dollars a year from the State of Maryland (750 million dollars over 15 years) plus roughly 10 million dollars a year from other state programs (UNVERIFIED by direct fetch; found via search snippet of a Maryland DHCD FY25 annual report PDF). The Green Network Plan, run by Baltimore's Office of Sustainability, is explicitly a GIS based plan to convert vacant lots into connected greened corridors and parks rather than treating each lot in isolation; two pilot projects, Rachael Wilson Park and Cab Calloway Legends Park, were dedicated by 2022 and are maintained by Baltimore's Department of Recreation and Parks (UNVERIFIED by direct fetch this session).

Lesson (lower confidence, none of the primary Baltimore sources were directly fetched this session): the one clearly transferable idea is scoring and connecting vacant lots as a network or corridor, not purely as isolated points, which would be a genuine enhancement over Clean & Green Philly's lot-by-lot ranking approach.

### Regrid (regrid.com) and parcel data licensing

Regrid is a commercial, nationwide parcel data aggregator, not an activist tool itself, but a likely data source for any of the tools above (VERIFIED, fetched regrid.com and the licensing details via WebSearch of regrid.com/purpose, 2026-10-04). It offers a free consumer lookup app, paid monthly API and data store plans for most customers, and, specifically relevant here, a "Data With Purpose" program for nonprofits, academics, researchers, and community organizations: a "pay what you can" model with either a Project License (single project) or a General Use License (broader use), where the organization states its budget and project scope and Regrid works out a price; applicants do not need official nonprofit status, only a non-commercial public benefit purpose (VERIFIED via WebSearch summary of regrid.com/purpose; UNVERIFIED by direct fetch of that specific subpage). Regrid also publishes some bulk "Standard Schema" data snapshots at no cost for research and academic use (UNVERIFIED by direct fetch). Whether Regrid's dataset specifically includes Philadelphia parcels was not confirmed this session (UNVERIFIED).

Lesson: for a Philadelphia-only tool, the City's own OpenDataPhilly parcel layer and atlas.phila.gov are almost certainly sufficient and free; Regrid becomes relevant mainly as a paid backup source, or for any future out-of-county comparison, and a modest "Data With Purpose" license would be a cheap insurance policy against another city-side data outage like the one that killed Clean & Green Philly.

## B. Land access paths in Philadelphia, as of October 2026

### 1. Philadelphia Land Bank

Official site: https://phillylandbank.org/ (VERIFIED, fetched 2026-10-04). The Land Bank lists eight disposition pathways: Side or Rear Yard, Community Garden/Open Space, Affordable Housing Development, Business Expansion, Assemblage, Community Use, Development RFPs, and Competitive Sales (VERIFIED, same fetch). It runs free public webinars; three were scheduled for October 6 to 8, 2026 covering the side yard, community garden, and land development application processes respectively (VERIFIED, same fetch). Contact: land@phdc.phila.gov, 215-448-3034 (VERIFIED).

Side or Rear Yard pricing and terms (VERIFIED, https://groundedinphilly.org/get-permission-to-use-land-city/, fetched 2026-10-04, cross-referenced with a WHYY article, see below): sold to the adjacent owner only, at a nominal, below-market price; the recipient must agree not to build on it; the land is permanently deed-restricted as a side yard, carries a 30-year mortgage at the lot's appraised market value, and the city holds a power of attorney allowing it to take the property back if the restriction is violated. Processing: Land Bank staff aim to respond within 120 days of application, after which the Land Bank board and then City Council must also approve the transfer (VERIFIED, https://whyy.org/articles/philadelphia-side-yard-land-bank-steward-union/, fetched 2026-10-04, dated April 21, 2026).

Community Garden License and related agreements (VERIFIED, groundedinphilly.org/get-permission-to-use-land-city/): four tiers exist. An Urban Garden Agreement costs a nominal $1 and can be canceled by the city at any time without notice. An Individual Garden Agreement costs $25 per year, lasts one year, and requires liability insurance. A Community Garden License lasts up to five years, and requires a nonprofit sponsor, liability insurance, a maintenance plan, and district council approval. An Interim Use Agreement is temporary, tied to a specific date or event, and must comply with the zoning code. Outright purchase requires the garden to have already operated for at least five years, plus a district council member's support. Important caveat found on the same page: "The Land Bank stopped entering into new lease agreements in 2020," which may constrain new garden licensing specifically; this should be confirmed directly with the Land Bank before telling users a multi-year lease is currently available.

Controversy (VERIFIED, WHYY, April 21, 2026, https://whyy.org/articles/philadelphia-side-yard-land-bank-steward-union/): side yard applicants and stewards describe the process as "slow and opaque." Only 67 publicly owned parcels were conveyed to residents for side yard use citywide between 2020 and 2023. A particular failure mode: a lot with unpaid back taxes can be sold out from under a long-time steward at sheriff sale (a separate, faster track, see below) while that same steward's Land Bank application is still pending, letting an outside, better-funded buyer outmaneuver the neighbor who did the work of greening it. A new "Philadelphia Land Steward Union" is organizing for a 180-day first-refusal window for neighbors before competitive bidding opens, eligibility extended to homeowners and long-term renters within 1,500 feet of a lot, publicly posted real-time acquisition statistics, on-site physical notice of pending development proposals, and dedicated application-processing staff.

Further reading (not independently fetched this session, UNVERIFIED): the Land Bank's formal acquisition and disposition policies, https://phdcphila.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/Land-Bank-Acquisition-Policies.pdf (dated 2019 in its filename, likely needing a currency check), and the city's Land Bank Strategic Plan at https://www.phila.gov/documents/land-bank-strategic-plan/.

### 2. Conservatorship under Pennsylvania Act 135

The Abandoned and Blighted Property Conservatorship Act lets a nonprofit corporation or an individual petition a court to be appointed conservator of a property, provided four conditions hold as of filing: the building has not been legally occupied for at least the prior 12 months; the owner has not actively marketed it or made a good faith sale effort within 60 days; it is not already subject to a pending private foreclosure; and the current owner did not acquire it within the last six months, except by family transfer (VERIFIED, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abandoned_and_Blighted_Property_Conservatorship_Act, fetched 2026-10-04).

Cost and incentive structure: an October 2014 amendment entitles a successful petitioner-conservator to a fee equal to the greatest of $2,500, a 20 percent markup on construction, rehabilitation, or demolition costs, or 20 percent of sale proceeds (VERIFIED, same source), a structure explicitly intended, per its sponsor, to make conservatorship "more attractive to private developers."

Controversy: a University of Pennsylvania Carey Law School study (Advocacy for Racial and Civil Justice Clinic) found Act 135 petitions are disproportionately filed in neighborhoods vulnerable to gentrification, and disproportionately against Asian American and, to a lesser extent, Black respondents (VERIFIED, same Wikipedia source, which cites Philadelphia Inquirer coverage from November 2023 and March 2024; the underlying Penn Carey Law report and Inquirer articles were not independently fetched this session). Reporting on the law describes a pattern of petitioners nominally filing as nonprofits while functioning as for-profit developers, since the nonprofit bar to file is easy to clear (UNVERIFIED by direct fetch, found via search snippet of 34th Street Magazine, April 2024).

Recent amendments: I could not verify any enacted amendment to Act 135 between 2023 and 2026 despite a dedicated search. What is confirmed is sustained legislative attention: Pennsylvania Senate and House Democratic policy committees held a hearing on Act 135 abuses as far back as March 26, 2021 (VERIFIED, https://senatorlindseywilliams.com/pa-senate-and-house-democrats-host-policy-hearing-on-needed-changes-to-abandoned-and-blighted-property-conservatorship/, fetched 2026-10-04), discussing proposals including faster sheriff sale procedures outside Philadelphia, greater use of land banks, and a "spot blight" eminent domain tool. A Philadelphia City Council hearing on Act 135's potential for harm is referenced in search results but was not independently fetched or dated this session (UNVERIFIED). Treat "Act 135 was recently reformed" as unconfirmed; what is confirmed is ongoing, multi-year scrutiny without a verified legislative fix as of this session.

Relevance to this project: because the 2014 fee structure financially rewards taking a property away from its owner of record, even through a nominally nonprofit petitioner, a public map that flags "12 months vacant, looks conservatorship-eligible" risks functioning as a lead list for exactly this documented abuse pattern. See the misuse section below.

### 4. PHS LandCare and Community LandCare

The Pennsylvania Horticultural Society (PHS) contracts with the City of Philadelphia, at roughly $2.5 million per year, to clear trash, add topsoil, install post and rail fencing, plant perimeter trees, and then mow and maintain vacant lots on an ongoing basis (VERIFIED via https://whyy.org/articles/philly-s-landcare-program-cleans-greens-and-reduces-crime/, fetched 2026-10-04, dated September 12, 2013). The program treats roughly 400 to 500 parcels a year, and sources differ on total scale: the 2013 WHYY piece cites 8,500 parcels under management, while a more recent search snippet (not independently fetched, UNVERIFIED) cites 12,000 lots covering 16 million square feet, described as "one third of Philadelphia's vacant land." This discrepancy was not resolved this session and should be rechecked with PHS directly.

Cost: the peer reviewed source of record (the Branas et al. randomized controlled trial, as summarized by the Department of Justice's own CrimeSolutions registry) gives an average upfront cost of $5 per square meter and $0.50 per square meter per year to maintain (VERIFIED, https://crimesolutions.ojp.gov/ratedprograms/741, fetched 2026-10-04, posted November 29, 2021). Other sources, not independently fetched this session (UNVERIFIED), translate this to roughly $1,100 to $1,500 per typical lot upfront and $150 to $300 per year to maintain.

Evidence strength: this is the best evidenced intervention anywhere in this report. The U.S. Department of Justice's CrimeSolutions registry rates the program "Promising, one study," based on a cluster randomized controlled trial by Branas and colleagues (2018), with follow up analyses by Moyer et al. (2019) and MacDonald et al. (2021) (VERIFIED, crimesolutions.ojp.gov). At an 18 month follow up: a 9.2 percent reduction in all crime, a 13.7 percent reduction in burglary, a 27.5 percent reduction in "nuisance" offenses, and a 2.4 percent reduction in gun assaults specifically. A 21 month follow up found a 10.6 percent reduction in shootings (6.8 percent per square kilometer). Robbery and theft showed no statistically significant effect, and drug offense results were mixed across studies (VERIFIED, same source).

Community LandCare, meaning how an individual resident or community group (rather than the city's large area contract) applies, what it costs that group, and current intake status, could not be verified this session: PHS's own site, phsonline.org, returned a 403 error on every attempt to fetch it. This is a real gap; recommend the team contact PHS directly or have a person visit phsonline.org, since this is one of the two interventions the project owner explicitly wants anchored (the other being the Branas evidence base above).

### 6. Buying from private owners

No dedicated Philadelphia program exists for this; it is an ordinary private real estate transaction. The Philadelphia specific complication, covered below, is that a very large share of lots that look privately owned and vacant cannot actually be sold by anyone, because the owner of record is deceased and the estate was never formally settled (a "tangled title"), which is precisely why items 8 and 9 below exist.

### 7. Sheriff sales

Process (VERIFIED, https://groundedinphilly.org/sheriff-sale/, fetched 2026-10-04, cross-referenced with search snippets of phillysheriff.com and the Philadelphia Sunday Sun, UNVERIFIED by direct fetch of those two): a property becomes eligible once it is roughly three or more years delinquent on taxes, water bills, L&I bills, or another lien. The city orders a Tax Information Certification from a title company, files a Sheriff Sale Petition in the Court of Common Pleas, and serves a Rule to Show Cause on everyone with an ownership interest. Absent payment in full or a payment agreement, the property sells at auction three to four months later. Bidding runs online through Bid4Assets.com; per search snippet (UNVERIFIED), a $500 deposit plus a $35 nonrefundable processing fee is required to register, and tax delinquent foreclosure lots reportedly open at $1,600. The winning bid clears all prior liens, so the buyer receives clean title regardless of whether the sale price covered the full debt (VERIFIED, groundedinphilly.org).

Risk for community groups (VERIFIED, groundedinphilly.org/sheriff-sale/): a group that has informally gardened a lot for years can lose it at sheriff sale to an outside, better capitalized bidder if the lot still carries old tax or water debt, exactly the dynamic the WHYY side yard article describes happening to Land Bank applicants as well. Grounded in Philly's own sheriff sale guide tells readers to check adverse possession eligibility first and cites the general 21 year rule; given the 2024 law described next, that guide may itself now be out of date for gardens specifically, worth flagging back to Grounded in Philly as a possible correction.

### 8. Adverse possession, including the 2024 Philadelphia garden law

General Pennsylvania rule (VERIFIED, https://groundedinphilly.org/how-to-obtain-land-adverse-possession/, fetched 2026-10-04): 21 years of continuous, actual, exclusive, open and notorious, and hostile possession (meaning without the owner's permission).

The 2024 change, specific to Philadelphia community gardens (VERIFIED, same source, which cites the statute directly): 53 Pa. C.S.A. Section 1143, effective September 6, 2024, cuts the period to 10 years of total occupancy, of which at least 5 years must be specifically as a garden. It applies only to Philadelphia resident individuals, groups, or nonprofits; only on privately owned land; capped at a half acre plot; no permanent structures, though fences and sheds are allowed; and claimants must keep "simple plans for managing work and paying taxes." Process and cost: this usually requires a lawyer, proceeds as a quiet title action in court, requires giving notice and then waiting a year before a claimant can even begin to prove the statutory elements, and any inherited tax debt or liens on the parcel remain the claimant's problem to resolve (VERIFIED, same source). Garden Justice Legal Initiative is the recommended contact for pursuing this (VERIFIED).

This is the single most significant, most recent, Philadelphia-specific legal change relevant to this whole project, and should be featured prominently rather than buried.

### 9. Tangled titles and the Tangled Title Fund

A "tangled title" describes a property where the deceased owner of record's estate was never formally settled, so no living person can prove clear ownership to sell, mortgage, get a repair permit, or access certain tax relief (general description; the specific framing "Billion Dollar Problem" comes from Hidden City Philadelphia and the scale description from a 2021 Pew report, both found via search, neither independently fetched this session, so UNVERIFIED by direct fetch though both are well known, credible sources worth the team re-confirming directly: https://hiddencityphila.org/2021/11/the-billion-dollar-problem-of-philadelphias-tangled-titles/ and https://www.pew.org/en/research-and-analysis/reports/2021/08/how-tangled-titles-affect-philadelphia).

The Tangled Title Fund (VERIFIED, https://phillyvip.org/tangled-title-fund/, fetched 2026-10-04): funded by the City of Philadelphia's Division of Housing and Community Development, administered by Philadelphia VIP. Grants up to $6,500 toward legal and administrative costs of clearing title, with up to an additional $3,500 case by case toward transfer tax costs. Income eligibility is capped at 80 percent of area median income (for example, $68,750 a year for a one person household, scaling to $129,600 for an eight person household, per the figures on the page). Non-home assets are capped at $20,000; the property must be the applicant's primary residence and not in dangerous condition; and the applicant must be able to cover any costs beyond the grant. Funding is first come, first served, subject to discretion of an independent Tangled Title Fund Advisory Committee of attorneys. To apply, contact Jordyn Bernard, TTF Administrator, at tangledtitlefund@gmail.com or 215-523-9553 (VERIFIED, same source). Four legal aid organizations implement the broader program: Philadelphia VIP, Community Legal Services, Philadelphia Legal Assistance, and SeniorLaw Center (UNVERIFIED by direct fetch of a page naming all four together; found via WebSearch summary of a cluster of consistent sources).

Relevance: a tangled title is frequently the real reason a property looks neglected in the first place, since the family living with or near it cannot legally sell, borrow against, or sometimes even repair it. The project should route a lot that turns out to have a tangled title toward the Tangled Title Fund and legal aid, not only toward greening interventions meant for lots with no legitimate occupant at all.

### 10. Deed theft and deed fraud

Scale (UNVERIFIED by direct fetch this session, consistent across multiple news outlets found via search including WHYY, PhillyVoice, and the Philadelphia Tribune): 111 reported deed fraud cases in Philadelphia in 2024. Fraudsters disproportionately target homes with elderly or deceased owners of record, and victims are disproportionately people of color, seniors, and residents of lower income neighborhoods.

Fraud Guard, a free deed monitoring alert (VERIFIED, https://www.phila.gov/2022-09-06-own-property-in-philadelphia-get-free-deed-fraud-protection-with-fraud-guard/, fetched 2026-10-04, dated September 6, 2022): free; sign up with name and email; sends an email roughly 30 days after any document naming the registrant is recorded; a parallel alert exists for notaries whose seal or signature appears on a new filing. Support line: 215-686-2290, records.info@phila.gov (VERIFIED). The specific signup URL (found via search, not independently fetched, UNVERIFIED) is epay.phila-records.com/phillyepay/eagleweb/fraudGuardSignup.jsp.

New automated deed fraud detection system (VERIFIED, https://www.phila.gov/2025-11-10-philadelphia-launches-new-tool-to-stop-deed-fraud/, fetched 2026-10-04, dated November 10, 2025): described by the city as the country's first such tool. It cross-checks every submitted deed transfer against the Social Security Administration's Death Master File and blocks recording if the named seller appears to have been dead at the time of signing. Built jointly by the Department of Revenue (which provides SSA data access), the Department of Records (which manages recordings), and the Law Department (which handles taxpayer confidentiality). I found no separate Register of Wills fraud alert program this session specifically; the verified programs (Fraud Guard and the new detection tool) both sit with the Department of Records, not the Register of Wills. This gap should not be treated as proof no such program exists, only that I could not find or verify one this session.

### Misuse risks and suggested safeguards

The project owner's instinct to worry about this is well founded, and the research above sharpens exactly where the danger sits. Four patterns recur:

First, the exact signal that makes a lot a good greening candidate (owner deceased or unreachable, property neglected, taxes delinquent) is also the exact signal deed thieves and Act 135 speculators look for. The city only closed this loophole for its own deed recording process in November 2025; a public map doing the same lookup and publishing it a few months later could function as a prioritized target list unless the project deliberately withholds or blurs that specific signal (for example, showing "ownership status unclear, contact Grounded in Philly" rather than "owner deceased, no estate filed").

Second, sale and ownership history is also exactly what a land speculator or an Act 135 petitioner-for-hire needs to find undervalued, under-defended parcels at scale, something a single public records search cannot do nearly as efficiently as a pre-aggregated citywide map can.

Third, the Land Bank's own process already shows that a community group's hard work greening a lot can be used as evidence the lot is "ready" for someone else, whether an outside developer racing the Land Bank's slow approval, or a sheriff sale buyer clearing old debt a steward never caused.

Fourth, showing a precise home address tied to a specific deceased person's name is itself a privacy exposure for any surviving family, independent of fraud risk.

Suggested safeguards, none of them independently verified against a legal opinion this session and offered as design recommendations rather than confirmed facts: delay or generalize ownership status for recently deceased owners rather than naming them; rate limit or require an account for bulk access to ownership and sale history rather than allowing one click, download everything; route "owner appears deceased or missing" cases to Grounded in Philly, the Tangled Title Fund, or legal aid rather than displaying raw status; make the tool's own terms of use explicitly prohibit speculative or conservatorship-for-profit use; and consider logging or rate-limiting high-volume scraping, the pattern that would distinguish an ordinary resident from a bulk speculator or data reseller.
