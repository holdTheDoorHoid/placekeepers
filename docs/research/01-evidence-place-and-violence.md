# The evidence on place based interventions and violence: what can honestly back a Philadelphia scoring tool

## Plain language summary

Philadelphia is the single best studied city in the world for this question, mostly because of one long running University of Pennsylvania research team. Their strongest finding, from a real randomized experiment, is that cleaning and greening blighted vacant lots cut nearby gun violence by around a quarter to a third, and a separate randomized trial found that fixing up abandoned houses (new doors, windows, cleanup) cut gun assaults by about 13 percent, both at low cost and with no sign that the violence simply moved next door. Good, though less dramatic, evidence also supports better street lighting. Evidence for trees in general, murals and public art, walkability, and transit is real but mixed: sometimes protective, sometimes null, sometimes it depends on context, and it is mostly correlational rather than causal. When other cities tried similar demolition or cleanup programs (Detroit, Cleveland, Rochester, New Orleans), the results were inconsistent: some found modest benefits, some found nothing, one found a small increase in crime near heavy demolition. So the Philadelphia results are real but should not be assumed to automatically repeat elsewhere or at larger scale. There are real risks to weigh too: greening and fixing up blocks raises nearby property values, which research on "green gentrification" warns can contribute to displacing the very residents the work is meant to help, and a public map of shootings risks stigmatizing neighborhoods or feeding over policing if it is read as a crime map rather than an investment map. Finally, Philadelphia's dramatic 2024 to 2025 drop in shootings is real and well documented, but credible local reporting attributes it mainly to policing changes and funded community violence intervention programs, not to greening or housing remediation, so a new tool should not claim credit for that citywide trend.

Every claim below is marked VERIFIED (I fetched the actual source this session) or UNVERIFIED (from general knowledge, a secondary summary, or a source I could not fully confirm). I was not able to confirm everything the project brief asked about, and I say so plainly where that happened rather than guessing.

## 1. Vacant lot greening

### The core Philadelphia research program, in order

These five papers are mostly reports on **two** underlying studies run by the same Penn/Branas led team, not five independent experiments. Keeping that straight matters for judging "replication."

**Garvin, Cannuscio & Branas, 2013** (the pilot). "Greening vacant lots to reduce violent crime: a randomised controlled trial." *Injury Prevention* 19(3):198 to 203. DOI [10.1136/injuryprev-2012-040439](https://doi.org/10.1136/injuryprev-2012-040439), PMID 22871378. **VERIFIED** (PMC full text and Europe PMC abstract fetched this session).
- Design: a true pilot RCT, but tiny: one intervention lot cluster and one control cluster. Resident interviews: 29 at baseline, 21 at follow up (10 intervention, 11 control completed both waves).
- Outcome: crime counts around the lots over a roughly 3.5 month pre/post window. Unadjusted difference in differences showed a net reduction in total crime and gun assaults, but this was **not statistically significant** (a chi square test on crime proportions, 31.2 percent vs 33.8 percent, was not significant).
- What was significant: residents near the greened lot reported feeling significantly safer (p less than 0.01), both unadjusted and regression adjusted.
- The authors' own conclusion was that this was promising but underpowered, and they explicitly called for a larger RCT. That larger RCT is the next entry.

**Branas, South, Kondo, Hohl, Bourgois, Wiebe & MacDonald, 2018** (the citywide trial). "Citywide cluster randomized trial to restore blighted vacant land and its effects on violence, crime, and fear." *PNAS* 115(12):2946 to 2951. DOI [10.1073/pnas.1718503115](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1718503115), PMID 29483246. **VERIFIED** (Europe PMC core record fetched this session).
- Design: cluster randomized controlled trial, three arms (full greening, mowing and trash cleanup only, do nothing control), 541 vacant lots grouped into geographically contiguous clusters, with crime outcomes from police data and a survey of 445 randomly sampled residents. Follow up: 38 months.
- Effect sizes reported in the abstract (comparing greening to control): overall crime down 13.3 percent (p less than 0.01), gun violence down 29.1 percent (p less than 0.001), burglary down 21.9 percent (p less than 0.001). Perceived outcomes: perceived crime down 36.8 percent, perceived vandalism down 39.3 percent, fear of leaving home down 57.8 percent, outdoor socializing up 75.7 percent (all p less than 0.05 or better).
- No confidence intervals were visible in the abstract text retrieved; the paper reports p values. This is the single strongest causal number in this entire evidence base for a violence focused scoring model.

**Moyer, South, Hohl, et al., 2019** (the shootings specific follow up, same trial). "Effect of Remediating Blighted Vacant Land on Shootings: A Citywide Cluster Randomized Trial." *American Journal of Public Health*. DOI [10.2105/AJPH.2018.304752](https://doi.org/10.2105/AJPH.2018.304752), PMID 30496003. **VERIFIED** (Europe PMC core record fetched this session).
- Same 541 lots and 110 clusters as Branas 2018, but the outcome is shootings specifically.
- Greening arm: shootings down 6.8 percent (95% CI: -10.6% to -2.7%). Mowing and trash cleanup arm: shootings down 9.2 percent (95% CI: -13.2% to -4.8%).
- No evidence of displacement of shootings to adjacent areas was found.
- Note the discrepancy with the 29.1 percent "gun violence" figure in Branas 2018: that larger number appears to be a different, broader gun violence measure (likely gun assaults generally) over a different sub-period, while this paper's 6.8 to 9.2 percent is specifically fatal and nonfatal shootings with a formal confidence interval. Both are real, they are just not the same outcome, which is exactly the kind of distinction a transparent scoring tool needs to preserve rather than collapse into one headline number.

**South, Hohl, Kondo, MacDonald & Branas, 2018** (the mental health follow up, same trial). "Effect of Greening Vacant Land on Mental Health of Community-Dwelling Adults: A Cluster Randomized Trial." *JAMA Network Open* 1(3):e180298. DOI [10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2018.0298](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2018.0298), PMID 30646029. **VERIFIED** (Europe PMC core record fetched this session).
- 442 residents enrolled across the same 110 clusters, 342 completed 18 month follow up (117 greening, 107 trash cleanup, 118 control).
- Greening arm: feeling depressed down 41.5 percent relative to control; feeling worthless down 50.9 percent; among residents below the poverty line, depression down 68.7 percent.
- Overall poor mental health: a nonsignificant 62.8 percent reduction. This is a useful cautionary example: a large percentage change that is still not statistically significant, almost certainly because the underlying event count was small. This is exactly the "small number noise" problem the scoring model needs to guard against (see Section D).

**Macdonald, Nguyen, Jensen & Branas, 2021/2022** (reanalysis of the same trial). "Reducing crime by remediating vacant lots: the moderating effect of nearby land uses." *Journal of Experimental Criminology* 18(3):639 to 664. DOI [10.1007/s11292-020-09452-9](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11292-020-09452-9). **VERIFIED citation** via CrossRef direct DOI lookup; abstract content not retrieved (paywalled), so the specific moderation findings are **UNVERIFIED** this session. The existence of this paper matters for Section 6 below: it is the group's own test of whether surrounding land use changes the effect of greening.

### Independent evidence synthesis

**Sadatsafavi, Sachs, Shepley, Kondo & Barankevich, 2022.** "Vacant lot remediation and firearm violence: a meta-analysis and benefit to cost evaluation." *Landscape and Urban Planning* 218. DOI [10.1016/j.landurbplan.2021.104281](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.landurbplan.2021.104281). **VERIFIED citation** (CrossRef). Pooled roughly eight interventions (about three mowing only, five greening, two gardening studies, per secondary search summaries). Secondary coverage reports greening reduced firearm violence by about 5.8 percent and gardening by about 5.3 percent pooled across studies, with cost savings recovering roughly 9 to 23 percent of program expenses. **These specific percentages are UNVERIFIED**: my direct fetch of the paper's own abstract returned empty, so I cannot confirm them against the primary source, only against search engine summaries of it. Treat the pooled percentages as plausible but not confirmed.

**CrimeSolutions.gov (Office of Justice Programs) program profile**, "Pennsylvania Horticultural Society's Vacant Lot Greening Program." **VERIFIED** (fetched this session). Official US DOJ rating: **"Promising"** (not the top tier "Effective"), based on the Branas 2018 / Moyer 2019 / Macdonald 2021 body of work. It separately reports no significant effect on robbery, theft, or illicit drug crimes in the Branas 2018 data. This is a useful, honest, independent calibration: the federal government's own clearinghouse does not call this "proven," it calls it "promising." The page also carries a notice that it is no longer actively updated.

### Other cities (greening/mowing specifically)

**Kondo, Hohl, Han & Branas, 2016.** "Effects of greening and community reuse of vacant lots on crime." *Urban Studies* 53(15):3279 to 3295. DOI [10.1177/0042098015608058](https://doi.org/10.1177/0042098015608058). **VERIFIED citation** (CrossRef); full effect sizes UNVERIFIED this session. Difference in differences study of Youngstown, Ohio's "Lots of Green" program (started 2010), matched treated lots against untreated controls.

**Heinze, Krusky-Morey, Vagi, Reischl, Franzen, Pruett, Cunningham & Zimmerman, 2018.** "Busy Streets Theory: The Effects of Community-Engaged Greening on Violence." *American Journal of Community Psychology* 62(1 to 2):101 to 109. DOI [10.1002/ajcp.12270](https://doi.org/10.1002/ajcp.12270). **VERIFIED** (full abstract retrieved via Europe PMC). Flint, Michigan's "Clean & Green" program. Mixed effects regression comparing 216 street segments with community maintained vacant lots to 446 segments with abandoned, unmaintained lots, 2009 to 2013 (5 years). Streets with maintained lots had **nearly 40 percent fewer assaults and violent crimes**, a result that held across 4 years of data. Important caveat the task asked me to flag: this is **observational**, not randomized. Communities that organize to maintain their own lots likely differ in other ways (social cohesion, existing collective efficacy) from communities that do not, so some of this effect could be selection rather than the greening itself. The paper's own framing (and a separate line of research cited in passing during my searches) suggests resident led maintenance may produce larger effects than professional maintenance alone, which if true is important for program design but is not a randomized finding here.

**Chicago Large Lots.** Stern & Lester, 2020/2021. "Does Local Ownership of Vacant Land Reduce Crime? An Assessment of Chicago's Large Lots Program." *Journal of the American Planning Association* 87(1):73 to 84. DOI [10.1080/01944363.2020.1792334](https://doi.org/10.1080/01944363.2020.1792334). **VERIFIED citation** (CrossRef) only. I could not retrieve the full text or abstract this session, so **I cannot tell you the direction of the finding**, only that this is the primary peer reviewed evaluation of the program (which sells city owned vacant lots to adjacent property owners for a nominal fee). The question mark in the title itself is a signal that the answer is not a simple yes. Do not assume this replicates Philadelphia's result; say "effect direction not confirmed" if this is cited anywhere downstream.

**New Orleans.** Kondo et al., approximately 2018, "Blight Abatement of Vacant Land and Crime in New Orleans" (USDA Forest Service hosted PDF; journal likely *Public Health Reports*, **UNVERIFIED** exact journal/volume because the PDF text did not extract cleanly). Context: New Orleans' "Fight the Blight" / Chapter 66 Vacant Lot Maintenance Program, launched 2014 with Tulane University, addressing blight partly caused by Hurricane Katrina. Per secondary search summaries (**UNVERIFIED** against the primary text): no significant difference in violent, property, or domestic crime pre to post remediation, but drug crimes near remediated lots fell about 5.7 percent relative to controls, concentrated in lots that received two or more treatments. This is an important **null finding on violent crime specifically**, from a city outside Philadelphia.

### PHS LandCare program: costs and current scale

**VERIFIED via multiple news and program sources fetched this session** (Shelterforce, WHYY, Wharton's Center for High Impact Philanthropy, PhillyVoice, phsonline.org), though the underlying dollar figures vary somewhat by source and year, which I report rather than smoothing over:
- Initial lot cleanup: approximately $1,500 per lot (Shelterforce/WHYY reporting). A separate academic cost figure used in the CrimeSolutions.gov research summary of the RCT itself was $5 per square meter initial, $0.50 per square meter per year maintenance, which is a research cost estimate for the trial intervention, not necessarily PHS's full real world program cost.
- Ongoing maintenance: reported figures range from about $300 per year (bi monthly mowing cost cited by one source) to about $1,100 per year per lot (a blended "clean and green plus maintain for a year" figure cited by Wharton's impact analytics page) to roughly 12 cents per square foot per year (phillyvoice/WHYY framing). These are likely measuring different scopes (bare mowing contract vs full program overhead including legal/title work), and I was not able to reconcile them to one number this session.
- City budget scale: sources differ between about $2.5 million per year (more recent city LandCare contract line item) and $6.5 million per year (an older figure describing the full PHS contract funded mostly by Philadelphia's General Fund and Division of Housing and Community Development, with under 1 percent from federal CDBG funds). Current program scale: PHS LandCare maintains **more than 12,000 vacant lots**, described as roughly 30 percent of the city's vacant lots, annually.
- Separately, **Community LandCare** (a resident stewardship arm distinct from the contracted mowing program) is referenced in passing in search results (a 2021 USDA Forest Service hosted paper on "resident led vacant lot greening") but I was not able to verify program level details this session; flag as **UNVERIFIED**.
- Property value effects: a Wharton/Lincoln Institute line of research (working paper by Desen Lin, 2026, hosted at the Lincoln Institute of Land Policy; **VERIFIED** that this paper exists and its basic framing, via direct fetch) is reported in press coverage (PhillyVoice, **UNVERIFIED** against the primary 53 page working paper text, which I could not extract) to find nearby property values rose about 4.3 percent in the first year and roughly 13 percent cumulatively after six years, within 1,000 feet of a greened lot, studying 4,651 greened lots against 16,799 untreated lots from 2007 to 2017. Press coverage also reports the effect is **not uniform**: it is larger in neighborhoods with higher than average income, higher than average vacant land share, and lower than average crime, which matters directly for the equity discussion in Section C.

## 2. Abandoned house remediation

**Kondo, Keene, Hohl, MacDonald & Branas, 2015.** "A Difference-In-Differences Study of the Effects of a New Abandoned Building Remediation Strategy on Safety." *PLOS ONE* 10(7):e0129582. DOI [10.1371/journal.pone.0129582](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0129582), PMID 26153687. **VERIFIED** (Europe PMC core record fetched this session).
- This evaluates Philadelphia's **Doors and Windows ordinance** (enforced starting 2011, requiring working doors and windows on abandoned buildings or fines).
- Design: difference in differences, Poisson regression, not randomized (a citywide policy rollout).
- Sample: 676 remediated buildings, 241 buildings permitted for renovation, compared against 676 non remediated and 964 non permitted control buildings. Period studied: January 2011 to April 2013.
- Remediation was associated with significant citywide reductions in overall crime, total assaults, gun assaults, and nuisance crimes (all p less than 0.001), and reduced violent gun crime in one city section specifically (p less than 0.01).
- **Important negative finding**: narcotics offenses and property crimes **increased** around remediated buildings (p less than 0.001). No spatial displacement was detected for the crimes that did fall.

**Branas, Kondo, Murphy, South, Polsky & MacDonald, 2016.** "Urban Blight Remediation as a Cost-Beneficial Solution to Firearm Violence." *American Journal of Public Health* 106(12):2158 to 2164. DOI [10.2105/AJPH.2016.303434](https://doi.org/10.2105/AJPH.2016.303434), PMID 27736217. **VERIFIED citation** (confirmed via Semantic Scholar this session); the actual cost benefit dollar figures were not retrievable from the abstract field this session, so those specific numbers are **UNVERIFIED**. The paper's premise, confirmed by its title, is a formal cost benefit analysis of blight remediation (both the Doors/Windows program and vacant lot work) against the cost of firearm violence.

**South, MacDonald, Tam, Ridgeway & Branas, 2022/2023.** "Effect of Abandoned Housing Interventions on Gun Violence, Perceptions of Safety, and Substance Use in Black Neighborhoods: A Citywide Cluster Randomized Trial." *JAMA Internal Medicine* 183(1), published online 2022, print January 2023. DOI [10.1001/jamainternmed.2022.5460](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2022.5460), PMID 36469329, PMCID PMC9857286. **VERIFIED** (Europe PMC core record fetched this session). This is the citywide cluster RCT on abandoned housing the brief asked about (note: I found it dated 2022/2023, not a separate unconfirmed "2023" paper; this is the correct study).
- Design: true cluster RCT, three arms (full remediation: doors, windows, cleanup; trash cleanup only; control), January 2017 to August 2020.
- Sample: 258 abandoned houses across 63 clusters, predominantly Black, low income neighborhoods.
- Full remediation vs control: weapons violations down 8.43 percent, gun assaults down 13.12 percent. Shootings specifically: a nonsignificant 6.96 percent reduction. Trash cleanup alone produced no significant differential effect. No displacement detected.

### Demolition studies outside Philadelphia (mixed and null results)

The brief asked specifically for "Detroit demolitions (Jay et al. 2019)." **I could not verify that this paper exists under that description.** I searched PubMed/Europe PMC and CrossRef directly for Jay plus Detroit plus demolition plus violence and found no match. What I did verify:

**Jay, Miratrix, Branas, Zimmerman & Hemenway (or similar author set), 2021/2022**, in **Rochester, NY**, not Detroit. "Effects of demolishing abandoned buildings on firearm violence: a moderation analysis using aerial imagery and deep learning." *Injury Prevention*. DOI [10.1136/injuryprev-2021-044412](https://doi.org/10.1136/injuryprev-2021-044412), PMID 34876475. **VERIFIED** (Europe PMC core record fetched this session). Grid cell analysis (152x152 meter cells), 2000 to 2020. One additional demolition was associated with a 14 percent reduction in shootings the following year (IRR = 0.86, 95% CI: 0.83 to 0.90). Effects varied by surrounding built environment density (a moderation finding, consistent with Macdonald et al. above).

**The actual Detroit demolition study I found**: Kagawa, Calnin, Smirniotis, Cerdá, Wintemute & Rudolph, 2022. *Preventive Medicine* 165(Pt A). DOI [10.1016/j.ypmed.2022.107257](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ypmed.2022.107257), PMID 36150449. **VERIFIED** (Europe PMC core record fetched this session). Studied roughly 2,600 demolitions across about 1,700 blocks in Detroit in 2017. **Null result**: crime risk, including firearm involved crime, in the quarters after demolition was statistically indistinguishable from what would have been expected had the demolitions not happened. This directly contradicts a simple "demolition always reduces violence" narrative, and the brief explicitly asked me to include null findings, so this belongs front and center for Detroit.

**Cleveland**: Smirniotis, Henderson, Bailey & Kagawa, 2022. *International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health*. **VERIFIED, full text fetched** (PMC9603059). Bayesian spatiotemporal Poisson models, 569 neighborhood sized hexagons, quarterly data 2012 to 2017 (24 quarters), 6,505 demolitions across 3,408 hexagon quarters. Nearly all credible intervals for relative risk included 1.0 (no significant effect). **Two exceptions showed increased, not decreased, crime**: high demolition with medium vacancy, relative risk 1.23 (95% CI: 1.01 to 1.48); high demolition with high vacancy, relative risk 1.07 (95% CI: 1.00 to 1.13). This is a genuine negative finding worth stating plainly: in Cleveland's data, heavy demolition in already high vacancy areas was associated with slightly *more* crime, not less.

**Baltimore**: Locke, Han, et al. (exact first author list not fully confirmed beyond "Locke"), 2023. "Vacant Building Removals Associated with Relative Reductions in Violent and Property Crimes in Baltimore, MD 2014 to 2019." *Journal of Urban Health*. DOI [10.1007/s11524-023-00758-3](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11524-023-00758-3), PMID 37526855, PMCID PMC10447335. **VERIFIED, full text fetched this session.**
- Design: difference in differences using spatio temporal Bayesian mixed models. 3,003 total building removals (2,567 demolitions, 436 deconstructions), 775 treated block faces vs 524 control block faces, 283,601 geocoded crime incidents, 2014 to 2019 (6 years).
- Total violent crime down 3.0 percent (95% credible interval: 1.9 to 4.1 percent); assaults down 4.9 percent (95% CrI: 3.4 to 6.3 percent, the largest single effect); burglaries down 1.94 percent (95% CrI: -3.83 to -0.02 percent, barely significant); total crime down 1.4 percent (95% CrI: 0.5 to 2.3 percent), about 2.6 fewer crimes per square mile per year.
- Effects were **not significant in the first three months** after demolition and only became significant over a full year, a useful note on follow up length. The study did not test for displacement, and could not separate demolition from deconstruction effects.

**Bottom line on replication across cities**: Philadelphia's own trials (RCTs) show the clearest, largest, most statistically confident effects. Quasi experimental replications elsewhere range from modest and real (Baltimore, Rochester) to essentially null (Detroit) to actively unfavorable in a subgroup (Cleveland, high vacancy areas). A scoring model should treat "demolition/remediation reduces violence" as well supported in Philadelphia specifically, and as plausible but inconsistent elsewhere, not as a universal law.

## 3. Street lighting

**Chalfin, Hansen, Lerner & Parker.** "Reducing Crime Through Environmental Design: Evidence from a Randomized Experiment of Street Lighting in New York City." *Journal of Quantitative Criminology*, published online 2021, print 2022. DOI [10.1007/s10940-020-09490-6](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10940-020-09490-6). **VERIFIED citation** (CrossRef). Design: a true randomized controlled trial run in New York City public housing developments, comparing developments randomly assigned additional lighting to those that were not. My session's searches consistently describe a statistically significant reduction in nighttime outdoor crime, but **I was not able to retrieve the exact effect size or confidence interval from the primary abstract this session** (PubMed/Europe PMC had no record, and the publisher page was not fetchable). Any specific percentage for this study circulating elsewhere (I have seen figures around a third reduction in nighttime outdoor index crime cited in secondary sources) should be treated as **UNVERIFIED** until confirmed against the paper itself.

**Welsh & Farrington, 2008.** "Effects of Improved Street Lighting on Crime." *Campbell Systematic Reviews* 2008:13. DOI [10.4073/csr.2008.13](https://doi.org/10.4073/csr.2008.13). **VERIFIED** via multiple corroborating sources fetched/found this session (Wiley Online Library listing, Campbell Collaboration site, OJP abstract page). Meta-analysis of 13 studies: a statistically significant overall reduction in crime of about 20 percent. Notably, the effect was larger in UK studies than US studies, and nighttime crimes did not fall more than daytime crimes, which the authors interpret as evidence that the mechanism is partly about signaling community investment and increasing community cohesion/informal social control, not simply better visibility for surveillance.

**An updated review** (title found: "The Impact and Policy Relevance of Street Lighting for Crime Prevention: A Systematic Review Based on a Half-Century of Evaluation Research," hosted on CrimRxiv) is referenced in search results as covering 21 studies across the US, UK, Brazil, and South Korea from 1974 to 2021. **I could not fetch this directly (blocked), so its existence and rough scope is reasonably well supported by multiple independent search snippets, but its specific findings are UNVERIFIED** this session.

I did not find Philadelphia, Chicago, or Detroit specific street lighting crime studies this session; I did not spend search budget on that narrower question given time constraints, so treat city specific lighting evidence beyond NYC as a gap, not a confirmed absence.

## 4. Trees, green space, parks and crime

**Troy, Grove & O'Neil-Dunne, 2012.** "The relationship between tree canopy and crime rates across an urban-rural gradient in the greater Baltimore region." *Landscape and Urban Planning* 106(3):262 to 270. DOI [10.1016/j.landurbplan.2012.03.010](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.landurbplan.2012.03.010). **VERIFIED citation** (CrossRef); the paper's specific finding (my recollection, not confirmed this session, is that the canopy to crime relationship reverses sign in some lower income urban core contexts, i.e. it is moderated by socioeconomic status) is **UNVERIFIED** this session since no abstract text was retrieved.

**Donovan & Prestemon, 2012.** "The Effect of Trees on Crime in Portland, Oregon." *Environment and Behavior* 44(1):3 to 30. DOI [10.1177/0013916510383238](https://doi.org/10.1177/0013916510383238). **VERIFIED, with abstract detail** (CrossRef returned a descriptive summary). Studied 2,813 single family homes, 2005 to 2007. Trees in the public right of way (street trees) were associated with lower crime. Trees on private lots showed a **mixed, size dependent effect**: smaller, view obstructing trees were associated with *increased* crime (they block sightlines), while larger trees were associated with *decreased* crime. This is a genuinely nuanced, non-uniform finding, useful for a scoring tool: "more trees" is not automatically protective, tree placement and size relative to sightlines matters.

**Kondo, Han, Donovan & MacDonald, 2017.** "The association between urban trees and crime: Evidence from the spread of the emerald ash borer in Cincinnati." *Landscape and Urban Planning* 157:193 to 199. DOI [10.1016/j.landurbplan.2016.07.003](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.landurbplan.2016.07.003). **VERIFIED citation** (CrossRef). This is a clever natural experiment: the emerald ash borer killed ash trees in a pattern unrelated to neighborhood social conditions, so the resulting *tree loss* can be used to infer the causal effect of trees on crime, rather than relying on where people chose to plant trees. I was not able to confirm the direction/magnitude of the result this session (no abstract retrieved); **UNVERIFIED** in detail, though the study design itself (funded in part by the CDC and USDA Forest Service per the metadata I did retrieve) is one of the more credible causal designs in this literature if the commonly reported direction (tree loss associated with more crime) holds.

**Green stormwater infrastructure, Philadelphia**: De Roos, Palmer, McIntire & Kondo, 2024. "Association of Green Stormwater Infrastructure Installations in Philadelphia with Resident Perceptions of the Neighborhood Social Environment." ISEE Conference Abstracts. DOI [10.1289/isee.2024.1548](https://doi.org/10.1289/isee.2024.1548). **VERIFIED citation** (CrossRef). Important caveat: this is a **conference abstract**, not a peer reviewed journal paper, and it measures resident *perceptions* of the social environment, not crime or violence directly. This is weak tier evidence and should not be used to justify a violence weighting for green stormwater infrastructure.

**Shepley, Sachs, Sadatsafavi, Fournier & Peditto, 2019.** "The Impact of Green Space on Violent Crime in Urban Environments: An Evidence Synthesis." *International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health* 16(24):5119. DOI [10.3390/ijerph16245119](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph16245119). **VERIFIED citation** (CrossRef): a PRISMA guided review that screened over 30,000 candidate titles down to 45 included papers on green space and violent crime. I was not able to confirm its bottom line conclusion from a retrieved abstract this session, so the overall direction of its synthesis is **UNVERIFIED**, though its existence and scope (the largest systematic review identified in this research) is confirmed.

I did not find and could not verify **Kuo & Sullivan, 2001** ("Environment and Behavior...does vegetation reduce crime?") or a Baltimore/Chicago specific parks and violence study this session; these remain **UNVERIFIED**, not confirmed absent.

## 5. Murals, public art, creative placemaking

**Tebes et al. (Yale School of Medicine, Program for Recovery and Community Health), "Porch Light Program: Final Evaluation Report."** Produced for Mural Arts Philadelphia and the Philadelphia Department of Behavioral Health and Intellectual disAbility Services (DBHIDS). Hosted at dbhids.org and Americans for the Arts. **VERIFIED to exist and VERIFIED general design** (fetched this session, though the PDF's internal text did not extract cleanly, so specific statistics below are **partially UNVERIFIED**).
- Document type: an internal/grant funded evaluation report, **not a peer reviewed journal article**. This matters: it has not been through academic peer review.
- Design: quasi-experimental, comparison group based, not randomized. Four year evaluation across eight Philadelphia neighborhoods. More than 1,300 street intercept interviews with residents, plus systematic block level observations. A separate behavioral health component compared 122 people enrolled in Porch Light against 142 in usual care at comparison agencies (264 total).
- Reported outcomes (per search summaries, **UNVERIFIED in exact statistical terms** against the primary PDF): increased "collective efficacy," improved perceived neighborhood aesthetic quality, and reduced stigma around mental illness and substance use among participants and residents.
- **What this evaluation does not appear to measure directly**: crime rates, graffiti, or an objective safety outcome with a comparable effect size to the studies above. The outcomes are predominantly self reported perception and psychosocial measures.
- **Honest evidence strength assessment**: this is the weakest tier of evidence in this report for a violence relevant scoring purpose: not peer reviewed, not randomized, and not measuring crime or violence as a primary outcome. It is reasonable evidence for murals affecting stigma and aesthetic perception; it is not evidence that murals reduce violence or crime.

I did not find any study linking murals or public art to crime, graffiti, or an objective safety outcome with a causal design. This is a genuine gap in the literature, not just a gap in my search.

## 6. Walkability, "eyes on the street," mixed land use, commercial activity

This is the one topic where the brief itself warns the evidence is mixed, and that held up.

- **Macdonald, Nguyen, Jensen & Branas, 2021/2022** (cited above under Section 1) directly tested whether nearby land use moderates the effect of vacant lot greening on crime; I could not confirm the direction of the moderation this session. DOI 10.1007/s11292-020-09452-9.
- **Wo, 2019.** "Mixed land use and neighborhood crime." *Social Science Research*. DOI [10.1016/j.ssresearch.2018.12.010](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ssresearch.2018.12.010). **VERIFIED citation** (CrossRef) only; direction UNVERIFIED this session.
- **Foster, Hooper, Burton, Brown, Giles-Corti, Rachele & Turrell, 2021** (Sage/health journal), on whether the association between neighborhood crime and walking differs by neighborhood disadvantage. **VERIFIED to exist via search** this session; full findings UNVERIFIED.
- General synthesis from multiple sources found this session: the evidence on walkability, "eyes on the street," and crime is **genuinely mixed** and mostly **correlational**, not causal. Several sources note that destinations which make a neighborhood walkable (shops, cafes) are also often the same destinations associated with crime risk (especially where they serve alcohol), so walkability and crime risk can rise together rather than trading off.
- **Alcohol outlets specifically** have a more consistent evidence base than walkability in general: multiple sources point to a real, fairly consistent association between alcohol outlet density and violent crime in the surrounding public space. This is a narrower, better supported claim than "commercial activity causes crime" or "walkability reduces crime."
- **Corner stores** play an ambiguous dual role in the literature I found: they can provide "eyes on the street" and serve as community hubs (one systematic review framed them as public health and social assets), but where they sell alcohol they inherit some of the alcohol outlet risk above. This is a case where the honest answer is "it depends what the store sells and how it is run," not a single direction.

**Recommendation for the scoring model given this mixed evidence**: walkability and generic "mixed land use" should not carry a violence weight. Alcohol outlet density specifically has better support as a risk indicator, if the owner wants a commercial activity factor at all.

## 7. Transit and violence

**Cerdá, Morenoff, Hansen, Tessari Hicks, Duque, Restrepo & Diez-Roux, 2012.** "Reducing Violence by Transforming Neighborhoods: A Natural Experiment in Medellín, Colombia." *American Journal of Epidemiology* 175(10):1045 to 1053. DOI [10.1093/aje/kwr428](https://doi.org/10.1093/aje/kwr428), PMID 22472117. **VERIFIED, full abstract fetched this session.**
- Design: natural experiment (not randomized, but using the staggered rollout of a specific infrastructure investment as a quasi-experimental comparison). The intervention combines the Metrocable aerial transit line (built 2004) with complementary public space and infrastructure investment in the neighborhoods it connected.
- Sample: 466 residents, 25 intervention neighborhoods vs 23 comparable control neighborhoods, baseline 2003, follow up 2008. Homicide data separately obtained from the public prosecutor's office.
- Homicide rate decline was **66 percent greater** in intervention neighborhoods than control neighborhoods (rate ratio = 0.33, 95% CI: 0.18 to 0.61). Resident reported violence decreased **75 percent more** in intervention neighborhoods (odds ratio = 0.25, 95% CI: 0.11 to 0.67).
- This is one of the strongest effect sizes with the tightest looking confidence intervals in this entire report, but note the design is a natural experiment in a very specific context (a city emerging from extreme cartel and paramilitary violence), so transferability to Philadelphia's very different baseline conditions is uncertain.

**Later Medellín evidence**: widely reported trend statistics (homicide rate falling from about 375 per 100,000 at the 1991 peak to about 52 per 100,000 by 2012, and continuing down, with a commonly cited "90 percent reduction 1991 to 2015" figure) are **VERIFIED as widely reported via search this session**, but these are citywide trend numbers, not a controlled estimate of what the transit and social urbanism investments specifically caused, separate from the broader end of the cartel war, national demobilization efforts, and other confounding history. The Cerdá 2012 paper above is the strongest causal isolation of the infrastructure specific effect that I could verify; the bigger "miracle" numbers should be presented as context, not as proof that transit investment alone caused them.

**Bogotá**: I could not find or verify a comparably rigorous evaluation of a Bogotá place based violence intervention this session. This is a gap, not a confirmed absence.

**Bus stops as crime generators/attractors**: I found this is a live, named concept in criminology (Brantingham & Brantingham's generator vs attractor framework), with recent (2026) reference chapters confirming the topic exists as a recognized research area (Elgar Encyclopedia of Transit Crime and Safety, chapters by Brantingham, Loukaitou-Sideris, Guedes, and Ceccato on bus stops specifically, their built environment, and "guarded" bus stops). **These are reference/encyclopedia chapters, not primary studies with effect sizes**, so I can confirm the concept and its names, not a quantified effect for lighting or shelters at stops specifically. That remains a gap.

## 8. Community cleanups, CPTED, and "broken windows": separating physical disorder from policing

This distinction is important and the evidence genuinely separates into two different bodies of work with two different conclusions.

**Physical disorder remediation** (the Branas/Kondo/South/Heinze line of work covered in Sections 1 and 2 above) has real, in several cases randomized, evidence that *fixing the physical environment itself* (greening lots, repairing abandoned buildings) reduces violence in Philadelphia specifically, with weaker and more mixed support elsewhere.

**Policing strategies aimed at disorder** are a separate body of evidence, and it draws a sharper, less flattering line:

**Braga, Welsh & Schnell** have a lineage of systematic reviews on this ("Can Policing Disorder Reduce Crime? A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis," originally in the *Journal of Quantitative Criminology* around 2015; a Campbell Systematic Review version, 2019, DOI [10.1002/cl2.1050](https://doi.org/10.1002/cl2.1050), PMID 37131517, **VERIFIED citation** via Europe PMC; and an updated 2024 version in *Criminology & Public Policy*, title "Disorder policing to reduce crime: An updated systematic review and meta-analysis," **VERIFIED to exist via search this session**, specific numbers UNVERIFIED).
- Consistent conclusion across versions, per search summaries this session: **community and problem solving strategies** that change physical and social disorder conditions at specific places produce a modest but real, statistically significant reduction in crime.
- **Aggressive order maintenance strategies** (zero tolerance style arrests for minor disorder offenses, targeting individual disorderly behavior rather than place conditions) **do not produce significant crime reductions**, and are separately documented to increase racial disparities in arrests.
- **Implication for the scoring tool**: this is a strong argument for keeping this entire project in the "fix the place" lane (greening, lighting, building repair) and explicitly not using it to justify or recommend increased aggressive policing or enforcement of minor disorder offenses. The evidence supports the former and does not support the latter, and the equity costs of the latter are separately documented (see Section C).

## C. Risks and equity

**Green gentrification**: Isabelle Anguelovski and collaborators have a substantial, mostly conceptual/qualitative body of work on this (confirmed this session via CrossRef, though I did not find a Philadelphia specific empirical Anguelovski paper):
- Anguelovski & Connolly, 2024, "Segregating by Greening: What do We Mean by Green Gentrification?" *Journal of Planning Literature*. DOI [10.1177/08854122241227804](https://doi.org/10.1177/08854122241227804). VERIFIED citation.
- García-Lamarca, Connolly & Anguelovski, 2020, on green gentrification and displacement in Barcelona (book chapter). DOI [10.4324/9780429427046-11](https://doi.org/10.4324/9780429427046-11). VERIFIED citation.
- Anguelovski, 2023 and 2025 book chapters on "(In)Justice in Urban Greening and Green Gentrification." DOIs [10.1007/978-3-031-22566-6_20](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-031-22566-6_20) and [10.4324/9781003430995-23](https://doi.org/10.4324/9781003430995-23). VERIFIED citations.
- The core argument across this body of work: greening can create "green gaps," where the amenity value of new or improved green space is captured disproportionately by already privileged, often whiter, higher income residents, while increasing displacement pressure on existing lower income residents of color nearby.

**Philadelphia specific evidence on this risk**: the Wharton/Lincoln Institute LandCare property value research described in Section 1 is the most directly relevant Philadelphia evidence I found: it shows nearby property values do rise after greening (reported, not independently confirmed in full, at roughly 4.3 percent in year one and 13 percent cumulatively after six years), and that this uplift is **larger in already higher income, lower crime neighborhoods** than in the most distressed ones. That heterogeneity is itself a displacement risk signal: the places most in need of violence reduction may see the smallest property value response, while greening in already improving areas may accelerate pressure that is already building. I also found a secondary reference (not independently verified) to a 10-US-city study finding new green space contributes to displacement of longtime lower income residents of color; I could not confirm this citation's exact details this session.

**Stigma and over-policing risk of crime mapping**: I found a substantial, directly relevant literature on predictive policing and algorithmic crime mapping (NAACP issue brief, Duke Law Journal, and academic papers on algorithmic fairness in policing, all found via search this session, **VERIFIED to exist**, general conclusions reported in search summaries). The consistent theme: crime data reflects **historical policing patterns**, not just underlying crime, so mapping tools built on it risk a feedback loop where already over-policed neighborhoods get flagged again, reinforcing both stigma and further enforcement attention. This is directly relevant here: a "nearby shootings" layer on a public map carries some of this same risk, **even though this project's purpose is to direct investment and greening, not policing**. Recommended mitigation: make clear in the tool's design and framing that any violence data layer is meant to direct caretaking resources (greening, lighting, building repair) to a place, never to direct police attention to people, and consider whether displaying raw point level shooting data (versus smoothed/aggregated area rates) increases stigma without adding decision-relevant precision.

**Placekeeping vs placemaking**: confirmed as a real, named distinction in the planning/public art literature (Planetizen, STEPS public art organization, Urban Institute, PolicyLink, all found via search this session). Placemaking generally refers to deliberately designing or building new activity and amenities in a place; critics note this can function as "beautifying gentrification," pulling in new users and new investment that eventually prices out existing residents and erases existing culture. Placekeeping is offered as a counter-principle: prioritizing continuity of existing community, culture, and control over a place, often paired with mechanisms like community land trusts that separate land value appreciation from the risk of displacement, since the trust (not an individual owner exposed to the open market) holds the land long term.

**Candidate displacement risk indicators a tool could surface** (this is my own synthesis from the above, not a single citation, offered as a design recommendation): current renter share on a block; recent sale price growth rate; eviction filing trends; share of nearby properties held by absentee or investor owners rather than owner occupants; proximity to a recently completed large scale investment or upzoning; demographic change in race/ethnicity and income over the preceding five to ten years; and, as a protective counter-indicator, the presence of a community land trust, deed restricted affordable housing, or long term resident ownership nearby. None of these are a causal measurement of displacement, they are flags that should prompt a human conversation with the affected community before intervening, not a formula that overrides local judgment.

### How an activist group can honestly evaluate its own intervention

1. **Use matched comparison lots, not before/after on the treated lot alone.** Pick nearby, similar, not yet treated lots or blocks (same council district, similar baseline vacancy and crime trend) as a comparison group, exactly as the Branas/South trials did with randomization and as Baltimore's Locke study did with matched block faces.
2. **Compute a difference in differences, not a raw before/after change.** Measure the change in the treated lot's area and the change in the comparison area over the same period, and compare the two changes to each other. A treated area that improves less than its own untreated comparison area has not shown a positive effect, even if its raw numbers went down.
3. **Watch for regression to the mean.** Lots and blocks often get selected for intervention precisely because they just had an unusual spike in crime. Unusual spikes tend to partially reverse on their own. Compare against areas with a similarly elevated recent trend, not just any convenient comparison area, or the "effect" may just be statistical reversion.
4. **Guard against small number noise.** Shootings are rare events at the scale of a single lot or block. A change from 3 incidents to 1 is a 67 percent "reduction" that is not statistically meaningful on its own; the South 2018 mental health trial shows a real world example of this (a 62.8 percent change that was still not statistically significant). Aggregate across as many treated sites as possible before computing a percentage, use confidence intervals or appropriate statistical tests (the published trials all pooled 60 to 110+ clusters for exactly this reason), and do not publish single lot percentage changes as if they were proven effects.
5. **Decide the follow up window before starting, and make it long enough.** The Baltimore study found no significant effect in the first three months, only after a full year; the Philadelphia RCTs used 18 to 38 month windows. A 60 day before/after comparison is not long enough to trust.
6. **Check for displacement to the next block.** Most of the studies above explicitly checked whether crime simply moved next door and mostly found it did not, but that was a deliberate analytical step, not something that happens automatically. Pull data for the immediately surrounding blocks too.
7. **Be honest about what design you actually have.** A single group before/after comparison (no comparison area at all) is the weakest possible design and is vulnerable to every problem above at once; a matched comparison area difference in differences is much stronger; a randomized assignment across many sites (only achievable at a program wide scale, not for one lot) is strongest. Say which one you are doing when reporting results.
