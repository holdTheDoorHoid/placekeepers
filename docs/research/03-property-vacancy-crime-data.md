# Philadelphia Property, Vacancy, and Crime Data: Auditing Clean and Green Philly and Mapping What Exists Today

Research date: October 4, 2026. Every claim below is marked VERIFIED (I fetched or queried the source myself this session) or UNVERIFIED (reported from a secondary source, general knowledge, or something I could not independently reach). A URL sits next to every claim. In line with the copyright rule for this report, only one short phrase anywhere below is reproduced word for word in quotation marks; everything else sourced from web pages, PDFs, or READMEs is paraphrased in my own words, with a citation.

## Plain language summary

Clean and Green Philly (CAGP) was a volunteer built website and data pipeline that helped Philadelphians find vacant lots worth cleaning up or acquiring, scored them by how urgently they needed attention, and explained how to legally get access to each one. It shut down at the end of July 2025. Its own shutdown letter gives one main reason, stated directly: "the City of Philadelphia no longer provides accurate vacancy data" (CAGP shutdown letter, July 1, 2025, read directly from the repository, VERIFIED), on top of being run entirely by volunteers without funding.

I rebuilt CAGP's full list of data sources by reading its code, then tested every endpoint it used, live, today. Most of the City's property, crime, and permit data is still there and still being updated daily or nightly: current OPA property records, a separate OPA assessment history table with 7.5 million rows, the Real Estate Transfer Tax ledger going back to 1974 (the real source for full sale history over time), L&I violations, complaints, permits, unsafe buildings, imminently dangerous buildings, and demolitions. Two things that CAGP depended on did break. First, the City's own per parcel tax delinquency table, which also carried a sheriff sale risk flag, is now access restricted; the public replacement only offers citywide aggregates by ZIP code, council district, or census tract, not individual parcels. Second, and most important: the exact Vacant Property Indicators layers CAGP relied on, which its own code treated as broken once their row counts fell below a hardcoded threshold, were bulk refreshed by the City on September 27, 2026, about a week before this research, and their row counts are now above CAGP's own thresholds. That is a genuinely new development since CAGP's shutdown letter. It does not by itself prove the data is accurate again, only that it exists and is fresh by the City's clock. The new project's first job should be to spot check it against known vacant and known occupied addresses before trusting it.

For a vacancy signal that does not depend on any single City table, the strongest combination is: OPA's own vacant land building code flag, L&I's Clean and Seal and Imminently Dangerous and Unsafe Buildings records with no permit activity afterward, demolition records with no new construction permit afterward, and the keyword filtered violations and complaints approach CAGP already built, cross checked against the City's own vacancy layer once it is verified, with tract level USPS or HUD vacancy data as neighborhood level context rather than a parcel level answer.

Full sale history, going back to 1974, with grantor, grantee, consideration amount, and document type (deed, mortgage, sheriff's deed, and so on), comes from the Real Estate Transfer Tax summary table, joinable to OPA by account number. Owner name and mailing address come from the live OPA properties table.

What I could not verify: the exact tile URL pattern PhilaGeoHistory uses, the literal license text for the HOLC redlining GeoJSON files, how property.phila.gov's own backend is wired (its page is a small client rendered app I could not trace further), and whether any City dataset literally dedicated to vacant property licenses exists on its own (I could not find one).

### A.2 The exact priority scoring logic

From `data/src/data_utils/priority_level.py` (read directly, VERIFIED). Inputs, computed earlier in the pipeline:
- Gun crime density z-score: z-score of a Gaussian kernel density estimate of gun related Part 1/2 crime incidents (see row 12 above) in a 0.25 mile grid.
- Violations past year: count of keyword filtered L&I violations on the parcel in the past year.
- L&I complaint density z-score: z-score of a kernel density estimate of keyword filtered 311/L&I complaints.
- Tree canopy gap: a 0 to 1 score of how much tree canopy is missing relative to potential, from the Tree Equity Score data.
- PHS care program flag: set if the parcel is in PHS's LandCare program.

The decision tree, transcribed exactly:
1. If gun crime density is at or below the citywide mean: **Low**, regardless of anything else.
2. If gun crime density is more than one standard deviation above the mean:
   - If there is at least one violation in the past year, or the complaint density is above the mean: **High**.
   - Otherwise, if the parcel is in PHS LandCare: **High** if the tree canopy gap is 0.3 or more, else **Medium**.
   - Otherwise (not in PHS LandCare, no violations, complaint density at or below the mean): **High**.
3. Otherwise (gun crime density between the mean and one standard deviation above it, a medium level):
   - If there is at least one violation, or complaint density is above the mean:
     - If in PHS LandCare: **Medium**.
     - Else, **High** if the tree canopy gap is 0.3 or more, else **Medium**.
   - Otherwise: **Low**.

In short: gun crime density is the dominant, almost gating variable; violations or complaints push a borderline parcel up; being in a PHS program can pull a parcel back down from High to Medium unless tree canopy is also very poor.

### A.3 Access process, side yard, conservatorship, and tactical urbanism logic

These are four separate columns, computed by four separate functions, not one combined decision tree. (`data/src/data_utils/access_process.py`, `city_owned_properties.py`, `conservatorship.py`, `tactical_urbanism.py`, all read directly, VERIFIED.)

- Access process (only computed for parcels already flagged vacant):
  - City owned by the Land Bank (agency equals Land Bank PHDC): result is `Go through Land Bank`.
  - City owned by the Redevelopment Authority (agency equals PRA): result is `Do Nothing`, CAGP's own label; PRA dispositions are handled outside CAGP's scope.
  - Not city owned, and market value above 1,000 dollars: result is `Private Land Use Agreement`.
  - Not city owned, and market value at or below 1,000 dollars, or null: result is `Buy Property`.
- Side yard eligibility: not derived logic at all, just a direct pass through of the City's own side yard eligible field on the LAMAAssets layer (row 5 above).
- Conservatorship (Pennsylvania's Act 135 conservatorship process):
  - False if the Land Bank owns it, or if it was not sold in the last 6 months and market value is above 1,000 dollars.
  - True if it has at least one violation in the past year, and it is not already flagged for sheriff sale by the (now access restricted) delinquency table, and it last sold more than 6 months ago.
  - False otherwise.
- Tactical urbanism: true only if the parcel type is land, and the parcel is not flagged as an unsafe building, and not flagged as imminently dangerous. A pure, three field rule.

### A.4 What broke, and how the final backup vacancy data was produced

CAGP's shutdown letter (`docs/PROJECT_BACKGROUND/C&GP Shutdown Letter, June 5, 2025.pdf`, actually dated July 1, 2025 inside the PDF; read directly, VERIFIED) states the organization ceased active development by the end of July 2025, citing two things together: the City no longer provided accurate vacancy data, and the project had outgrown what an all volunteer team could sustain without funding.

The mechanism, visible directly in the code: `vacant_properties.py` pulls the two ArcGIS layers in row 2 and row 3 above, and if either comes back under its hardcoded threshold, it silently drops that layer's rows and substitutes a local backup file instead. `data/backup_data/README.md` (read directly, VERIFIED) explains the backup's provenance:
- `land_backup_2024_06_24.parquet`: vacant land data that the Department of Licenses and Inspections sent to CAGP directly, the README describes it as the last reasonably complete vacant land dataset before the City's indicators broke, and links to a June 25, 2025 Inquirer opinion piece about the Mayor's housing plan missing this data (`https://www.inquirer.com/opinion/commentary/mayor-parker-housing-plan-missing-data-20250625.html`, cited in the README; I did not independently fetch the Inquirer article this session, UNVERIFIED beyond the citation itself).
- `buildings_backup_2024_06_24.parquet`: vacant buildings data CAGP collected itself in June 2024, which the README candidly describes as likely missing on the order of a thousand or more buildings, because the team did not realize at the time that the City's buildings dataset was also already corrupted.
- Combined, about 34,000 properties, frozen at June 2024, and hardcoded as the fallback whenever the live layers look too small.

Separately, and not mentioned in CAGP's own documentation because it postdates the project, the parcel level tax delinquency table (row 14 above) is now access restricted, which independently would have broken the sheriff sale flag CAGP's conservatorship logic depends on, and the per parcel detail CAGP's delinquency display depended on. See Section B for the public replacement, which is aggregated only.

### A.5 Infrastructure, and what it would cost or take to run

(`data/Dockerfile`, `data/docker-compose.yml`, `data/pyproject.toml`, `docs/TECHNOLOGIES.md`, `docs/SETUP/BACK_END.md`, `data/src/classes/loaders.py`, `slack_reporters.py`, `bucket_manager.py`, all read directly, VERIFIED.)

- No self hosted database, no PostGIS. CAGP never ran its own Postgres/PostGIS instance. It queries the City's own Carto SQL API and ArcGIS FeatureServers live on every run, paginating itself (a Carto loader fetches in 100,000 row chunks across a thread pool; an Esri dumper pages at 2,000 features per request with a 1 second pause every 10 requests), and holds everything in memory as GeoPandas frames.
- Compute: a Python 3.11.4 pipeline, dependency managed with `uv`, containerized with a three stage Docker build (a stage that compiles tippecanoe from the felt/tippecanoe fork, a dependency stage, and a final GDAL enabled runtime image). `docs/TECHNOLOGIES.md` says it runs on a scheduled Google Cloud Compute Engine VM. A real, timestamped run logged in `docs/ETL pipeline/DataDict.md` took about 19 minutes end to end (2025-03-20, 01:45:03 to 02:04:45); the single slowest step by far was the L&I complaints kernel density estimate at 645 seconds, about 10.75 minutes, versus 20 to 60 seconds for most other steps.
- Storage and tiles: a public Google Cloud Storage bucket, named cleanandgreenphl (confirmed by reading the frontend source, `src/config/config.ts` and `src/components/PropertyMap.tsx`, VERIFIED), holds per parcel Street View style JPGs keyed by OPA ID, the PMTiles file, and timestamped diff and backup reports. Tiles are built with tippecanoe (points layer zoom 10 to 13, polygons zoom 13 to 16, merged with tile-join) and served straight to the browser; MapLibre GL fetches them client side via the pmtiles protocol, so there is no tile server to run at all.
- Reporting: a Slack bot posts dataset QC profiles, diff reports between runs, and pipeline errors to two channels; email reports are also configurable but not detailed further in what I read.
- Frontend: Next.js 14, React 18, TypeScript, Tailwind CSS v3, MapLibre GL JS 5.2 via react-map-gl 8, MapTiler for the basemap and geocoding control (needs its own API key), confirmed from `package.json` (VERIFIED). The old prototype URL named in the docs, `https://vacant-lots-proj.vercel.app/`, now returns HTTP 404 (VERIFIED, tested this session), so that specific deployment is dead; I could not confirm where the real production site is hosted from the repository alone (UNVERIFIED).
- Cost estimate (my own estimate, not a verified bill): given no managed database and a pipeline that runs for well under an hour, the dominant recurring cost would be Cloud Storage for roughly 580,000 small JPGs plus the tile and parquet files, which is very likely tens of dollars a month, not hundreds. The real cost of this project, consistent with its own shutdown letter, was volunteer engineering time, not cloud infrastructure.

### A.6 Lessons learned, and what is worth reusing

Worth reusing directly:
- The one function per indicator shape, each with its own input and output validator (`data/src/validation/*.py`) and a generated data dictionary and DAG document. It makes the whole pipeline legible and testable.
- PMTiles generated by tippecanoe and served straight from public object storage to MapLibre GL in the browser. No tile server, cheap, and it held up; worth copying wholesale.
- Keeping a last known good backup plus an automatic threshold check. The instinct is right even though the implementation, count only, no date check, was not enough to catch the actual failure mode early.
- The Slack backed QC profile and diff report habit, cheap, and it is exactly the kind of thing that would have surfaced the vacancy data's collapse earlier if someone had been watching the channel.
- Centralizing every data source's license and provenance in one document.

Worth fixing, not copying as is:
- The staleness check should test a date, not just a row count; a bulk reload with a uniform timestamp, exactly what I found happened on September 27, 2026, would pass CAGP's threshold check without actually proving the data is trustworthy.
- Owner type and city owner agency are assigned by matching hardcoded owner name strings and mailing addresses in `city_owned_properties.py`; it works today but will silently miss anything the City renames or re-addresses.
- CAGP never actually surfaced historical sale data to users, even though the Real Estate Transfer Tax table that would answer what a property sold for historically was sitting right there, live, and public the whole time; the current OPA properties table only ever exposed the single most recent sale. That gap is exactly what this new project is being asked to close.
- The gun crimes signal is a proxy built from two Part 1/2 incident categories (aggravated assault with a firearm, robbery with a firearm), not the City's own purpose built Shooting Victims dataset (Section D), which would be a more precise and defensible signal for a gun violence focused product.
- Internal documentation drifted from the code in more than one place (the Park Priority source link in the data dictionary points at a generic Trust for Public Land page rather than the specific ArcGIS FeatureServer the code actually hits; the PHS LandCare layer names in the same document are dead, as noted above). Generating docs from the code, rather than maintaining them by hand, would have avoided both.

## Section C: Rebuilding a vacancy signal without the City's indicator

What exists, each independently usable today:

- OPA land use and building codes (`opa_properties_public`, Section B): the building code description containing the words vacant land is literally how CAGP itself derives its own land versus building classification in `opa_properties.py` (VERIFIED, read directly). Always live, always current, but only captures parcels OPA has already classified as vacant land; it will not catch a vacant building.
- L&I vacancy related violation and complaint codes: CAGP's own keyword list, read directly from `services.py` (VERIFIED): dumping, blight, rubbish, weeds, graffiti, abandoned, sanitation, litter, vacant, trash, unsafe, applied to both the violations table and the complaints table. Both tables are live today (Section B). L&I Clean and Seal and Imminently Dangerous Buildings records are a stronger, more specific signal: a property the City physically sealed, or declared imminently dangerous, is a high confidence vacancy or near vacancy indicator, especially if no building permit has been issued since, since a subsequent permit is a good sign the property was reoccupied or renovated.
- Vacant property licenses: not found as their own dataset (Section B); the new L&I Property History dataset (`https://opendataphilly.org/datasets/licenses-and-inspections-property-history/`, VERIFIED to exist, contents not yet explored in depth) is a promising unverified lead worth querying directly before building anything else.
- USPS vacancy data via HUD: HUD has bought quarterly, ZIP plus four level vacancy and no-stat address counts from USPS since 2005, which HUD then aggregates up to the census tract level before releasing it; about 1 percent of records cannot be geocoded and are dropped (paraphrased from `https://www.huduser.gov/portal/periodicals/cityscpe/vol23num3/ch11.pdf`, reported from search results, UNVERIFIED by direct fetch). Access is restricted by HUD to registered government and nonprofit users, via `https://www.huduser.gov/apps/public/usps/login` (UNVERIFIED by direct fetch, login gated by design). This is neighborhood level context, not a parcel level answer, and would need a registered partner, such as a city department, a university, or a nonprofit, to obtain.
- Water account or shutoff data: I found no public, open dataset for PWD account status or service shutoffs (not found in the OpenDataPhilly catalog search, UNVERIFIED as confirmed absent, only confirmed not found this session). Treat this as a stretch goal requiring a direct data sharing relationship with PWD, not something available as open data today.
- Aerial imagery and vegetation indices: the City publishes its own Aerial Photography orthophoto dataset with yearly vintages back to 1996: 1996, 2000, 2004, 2005, 2008, 2009, 2010, 2012, 2014, 2015, 2016, 2017, 2019, 2020, 2022, and 2023, each with TIFF downloads, a tile index, and an ArcGIS Online imagery layer (`https://opendataphilly.org/datasets/aerial-photography/`, VERIFIED, read the full resource list directly). A multi year, same parcel comparison of greenness, a simple vegetation index computed from the imagery, is a credible, free signal: a lot that stays green and low over several years reads very differently from one with a standing structure. NAIP, the USDA's National Agriculture Imagery Program, is a nationwide, public domain aerial source that could supplement years the City's own archive does not cover, though I did not test a specific access URL this session (UNVERIFIED, reported from general knowledge, not fetched).
- Demolition records: live, daily, since November 2017 (Section B). A demolition with no subsequent new construction permit is a strong, low cost vacancy signal.
- Tax delinquency: parcel level detail is gone (Section B); the aggregated ZIP, council district, and census tract statistics that remain are only useful as area level context, not a parcel level flag.
- How CAGP built its own backup: a direct data transfer from L&I for land, and manual, self collected data for buildings (Section A.4), a real but non scalable technique; a formal data sharing agreement with OPA or L&I, rather than relying solely on the public API, is the durable version of the same idea.
- Published methods for predicting vacancy: a Philadelphia specific random forest model was built to predict vacant land price for the Philadelphia Land Bank by Ken Steif's Urban Spatial Analysis practice (`https://urbanspatialanalysis.com/portfolio/predicting-vacant-land-price-for-the-philadelphia-land-bank/`, reported from search results, UNVERIFIED by direct fetch); a paper on predictive analytics for proactive vacancy policy is explicitly Philadelphia focused (`https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0040162513002138`, UNVERIFIED by direct fetch); a vacant lot conversion predictor called LotRec was tested on both Baltimore and Philadelphia data using k-nearest-neighbors and random forest classifiers (`https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.02481`, UNVERIFIED by direct fetch). CAGP's own foundational citation, Dr. Eugenia South's PNAS study on greening vacant lots and reducing gun violence, is not a vacancy predictor but is the research basis for the whole project (`https://www.pnas.org/doi/full/10.1073/pnas.1718503115`, read directly from `docs/PROJECT_BACKGROUND/PROJECT_BACKGROUND.md`, VERIFIED).

Recommended multi signal approach, with confidence levels, modeled on CAGP's own priority level pattern but applied to the vacancy determination itself rather than assuming it:
- High confidence: the OPA building code description says vacant land, or an L&I Clean and Seal or Imminently Dangerous Buildings record exists with no permit issued since.
- Medium confidence: elevated keyword filtered violation or complaint density (CAGP's existing approach), or a demolition record with no subsequent new construction permit.
- Medium confidence, pending verification: the City's own Vacant Property Indicators layers, now live and fresh as of September 27, 2026 (Section A.1), promoted to high confidence only after the new project independently spot checks a sample of addresses.
- Low confidence, contextual only: PWD impervious area anomalies, multi year aerial imagery vegetation trends, and tract level USPS or HUD vacancy rate as a neighborhood prior.

Surface the number and type of agreeing signals to users directly, the way CAGP surfaced its priority level, rather than collapsing everything into one opaque label.
