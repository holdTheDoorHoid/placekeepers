# How we find vacant land

The City used to publish its own list of vacant lots and buildings, but that list stopped being
reliable in June 2024, and that is the reason the original Clean & Green Philly project closed.
Placekeepers never leans on just one list. We combine the City's own list with several other
public records, so that one broken source cannot take the whole map down.

## What you will see on the map

Every parcel we believe is vacant shows up as either a **lot** (no building standing on it) or a
**building** (standing, but empty), along with how confident we are: **high**, **medium**, or
**low**. Each one shows its reasons in plain words, for example "the City's list says this is
likely vacant land, the City's property records classify it as vacant land, and there is no
building footprint on the parcel." High and medium confidence parcels show by default; low
confidence parcels are hidden behind a filter, because they are little better than a coin flip.
Parks, gardens, parking lots, rail lines, utilities, and cemeteries never show as vacant, no
matter what any list says.

|---|---|---|---|---|

## What we found

- **The City's September 2026 list is a real update, and it is mostly right about lots.** When
  the City's list and at least two of our own independent records agree a lot is vacant, every
  one we checked against aerial photos really was empty.
- **The City's list by itself is not enough.** When only the City's list says a lot is vacant,
  with none of our other records agreeing, fewer than half of the ones we checked against photos
  were actually empty. The rest were houses, parking, or even a playground.
- **Our own records find about 4,200 more vacant lots that the City's list leaves out.** Most of
  these look like real vacant lots once we checked them.
- **The City's list is slow to catch new vacant lots created by tearing a building down.** A
  demolition shows up in our own records right away; it can take the City over a year to add the
  resulting lot to its own list.
- **Buildings are harder to judge than lots.** A photo from above cannot tell whether a standing
  building is lived in. For buildings, we can only measure how well different City records agree
  with each other, which is weaker evidence than a photo.

## How we decide

- **High confidence:** the City's list agrees, and at least two of our own independent records
  agree too (for example, no building footprint, a demolition with nothing built since, or a
  known vacant lot violation), and nothing contradicts it.
- **Medium confidence:** strong agreement, but with one thing that gives us pause, such as a City
  planning map showing some other use, or a new construction permit. We also give medium
  confidence to some lots the City's list does not include at all, when at least two of our own
  records clearly agree they are vacant.
- **Low confidence:** only one piece of evidence, or evidence that contradicts itself. These sit
  behind a filter because they are close to a coin flip, and we would rather hide a guess than
  show it as fact.

## If the City's list breaks again

We will keep the last good copy of the City's list for twelve months, clearly labeled with its
date. After that, or sooner if it stops being updated the way it should, we will switch to rules
that do not depend on it at all. The map would say less with full confidence: most lots that are
high confidence today would drop to medium (keeping roughly 29,000 lots on the map at medium
confidence overall), while about 2,936 buildings would stay at high confidence. The map would
never go fully dark. It would simply say less with certainty, and say so plainly.

## How sure we are

We checked a small sample of parcels against the City's own aerial photographs from spring 2023,
without knowing in advance which list each parcel came from, so the check would be fair. For lots
we call high confidence, 24 to 26 out of 26 checked were truly empty. For medium confidence lots,
about 18 to 20 out of 23 were truly empty. For low confidence lots, it really was close to a coin
flip, which is exactly why they stay hidden by default.

We could not check buildings this way, because a photo from above cannot show whether anyone
lives inside. A person still needs to walk by a short list of addresses and look, and we will add
those results to the map once that happens.

This check was small (115 parcels in total), done by one reviewer looking at photo crops, using
photos that are already a few years old. We treat it as a reality check on our rules, not as
final proof, and we will keep improving it.
