# Why this works

Philadelphia is the most studied city in the world on this question. Two separate, carefully
controlled trials, right here, found that fixing neglected places reduces gun violence nearby.
This page explains what the research actually found, in plain words, what it does not show, and
the risks we design for. The full research notes are on GitHub in `docs/EVIDENCE.md`, with links
to every paper.

## The two Philadelphia trials

**Cleaning and greening vacant lots.** Researchers (Branas and colleagues, published in the
journal *PNAS* in 2018) picked 541 vacant Philadelphia lots at random and split them into three
groups: full greening (clearing trash, grading the ground, planting grass and a few trees, and a
low wooden fence, with regular mowing after), cleanup only, or nothing. They tracked what happened
for 38 months.

In neighborhoods below the poverty line, the greened lots saw gun violence fall by 29.1%, all
crime fall by 13.3%, burglary fall by 21.9%, and nuisance crimes fall by 30.3%. Neighbors living
near greened lots reported feeling 36.8% safer from crime, 57.8% more comfortable going outside,
and spent 75.7% more time relaxing and socializing outdoors.

A second study of the same trial measured shootings on their own and found them down 6.8% near
greened lots (researchers are confident the true drop is somewhere between 2.7% and 10.6%), and
down 9.2% near lots that were only cleaned and mowed, without full greening (confidently somewhere
between 4.8% and 13.2%). There was no sign that shootings simply moved to the next block. A third
study found neighbors near greened lots reported feeling depressed 41.5% less often than neighbors
near untreated lots, and 68.7% less often among neighbors below the poverty line.

**Fixing abandoned houses.** A separate trial (South and colleagues, published in *JAMA Internal
Medicine* in 2023) picked 258 abandoned houses, mostly in Black, lower income neighborhoods, and
randomly assigned them to full repair (working doors and windows, plus cleanup), cleanup only, or
nothing. Full repair cut gun assaults by 13.1% and weapons violations by 8.4%. Shootings fell
7.0%, though that smaller number was not large enough for researchers to rule out chance. Cleanup
alone, without the repairs, made no measurable difference. Nobody was displaced because of the
repairs.

An earlier study of Philadelphia's rule requiring working doors and windows on vacant buildings
found drops in assaults after it was enforced, though property crime nearby went up at the same
time.

Both projects are cheap. Both worked best in the poorest neighborhoods. Neither pushed the problem
onto the next block.

## How strong is the evidence

Every score and suggestion on the map carries a badge that says how strong its evidence is, so you
can judge for yourself how much weight to give it.

| Badge | What it means |
|---|---|
| Strong | A randomized trial, ideally here in Philadelphia, found the effect. Greening a vacant lot and fixing an abandoned house both carry this badge. |
| Moderate | Careful before and after studies with a comparison group found the effect. Street lighting carries this badge: a review of 13 studies found crime down about 20% after lighting improved. |
| Mixed | Good studies disagree, or the effect depends on how something is done. Trees carry this badge: big trees are linked to less crime, but small trees on private lots that block sightlines are linked to more. |
| Weak | Only surveys, opinions, or one small study support it. |
| Not about violence | The thing is worth doing for other reasons, but no study links it to less violence. Murals carry this badge: they are linked to neighbors feeling more pride and connection, but no study has tied them to less violence, so murals live in their own lens, not the violence score. |

Walkable streets and crowded sidewalks are not in this table because the research cuts both ways;
busier streets can mean more watchful neighbors, or more places that sell alcohol, which is its
own risk factor. How many people live nearby is never used to raise or lower a violence score. We
only use it to say how many people a project would reach.

## What we do not claim

**We do not claim credit for the citywide drop in killings.** Philadelphia recorded 562 homicides
in 2021, 410 in 2023, 269 in 2024, and 222 in 2025, the fewest since 1966. That drop is credited
mainly to changes in policing and to roughly $600 million spent on violence prevention, including
direct work with people at the highest risk. Greening and repair work block by block. It does not
explain the citywide trend, and we never say it does.

**We are not about policing.** Studies of "fixing places and solving problems with neighbors"
find it modestly reduces crime. Studies of arresting people for minor offenses find it does not,
and that it falls unevenly on Black and Hispanic residents. Placekeepers is about caring for
places. It is never about directing police toward people.

## The risks we design for

**Rising prices.** Research on PHS's lot greening program finds nearby property values tend to
rise afterward, more so in places that were already doing better. Researchers are still confirming
exact numbers. A greener block can be a more expensive block, which can push out the very
neighbors it was meant to help. That is why the map includes a displacement watch layer, and why
suggestions are paired with protections such as the Neighborhood Gardens Trust wherever this risk
applies. Read more on [Use this responsibly](https://holdthedoorhoid.github.io/placekeepers/responsibly/).

**Being seen as "dangerous."** A map of shootings can be read as "stay away from here" instead of
"this place deserves investment." That is why Placekeepers shows shootings as counts across an
area of about two blocks, not as a list of exact points, and why it never ranks neighborhoods by
danger.

## How to tell if your project worked

1. Compare your place with similar places you did not treat, not only with how your own place
   looked before.
2. Compare the change at your place with the change at the comparison places, over the same
   stretch of time.
3. Watch out for picking a place right after a bad spike. Spikes often fade on their own.
4. Shootings are rare events. One place going from 3 to 1 is not proof of anything. Look at many
   places together before claiming a percentage, and say how confident you really are.
5. Decide how long you will wait before judging, and make it at least a year. A similar study in
   Baltimore saw nothing at all in the first three months.
6. Check the blocks around your project too, in case a problem simply moved there.
7. Say plainly what kind of comparison you made, so other people can judge for themselves.
